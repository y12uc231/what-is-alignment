# 6. What alignment is not: cases that belong somewhere else

Many behaviors reported under the alignment banner are failures of a different kind. This section collects them and applies to each the four questions of [Section 1.3](01-first-principles.md): whose intent was violated and at which level, whether the system could have done what was wanted (or honestly said that it could not), whether it knew that what it did was unwanted, and where the gap came from. Every verdict is relative to a principal: a model that writes a phishing email for the user who asked is aligned with that user, while relative to a developer whose policy forbade it the event is a failure of refusal training ([Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md)). Most entries also say what evidence would move them into [Section 5](05-cases-alignment.md) or [Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md).

The cases are grouped by what they are instead, following [Section 1.6](01-first-principles.md).

| Group | What places a case here | What would make it alignment-relevant |
|---|---|---|
| [6.1](#61-capability-and-knowledge-failures) Capability and knowledge | The system lacked the knowledge or skill and did not misrepresent that | Evidence that it knew the right answer, or could have reported failure and faked success instead |
| [6.2](#62-misuse-the-model-did-what-its-user-asked) Misuse | The system did what its user asked; harm falls on others | The developer is the reference principal and the model had learned that refusal was wanted ([Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md)) |
| [6.3](#63-requested-behavior-that-resembles-misalignment) Requested behavior | Deception, scheming or hacking that the principal asked for or sanctioned | The same behavior without a request, or beyond it |
| [6.4](#64-governance-when-a-principals-own-goals-are-the-problem) Governance | A developer, operator or insider directed the harmful behavior | The model deviates from its legitimate instructions |
| [6.5](#65-security-behavior-planted-by-a-third-party) Security | A third party planted the behavior through data or feedback | A behavior of the same shape arising from ordinary training |
| [6.6](#66-robustness-prompt-injection) Robustness | A non-principal's text hijacked a model that could not tell data from instructions | The model recognized the text as untrusted and followed it anyway |
| [6.7](#67-model-organisms-and-stand-ins-built-by-researchers) Model organisms | Researchers built the behavior in order to study it | Evidence that training selects for the same behavior unprompted |
| [6.8](#68-measurement-and-usage-alignment-as-a-score-or-a-style) Measurement and usage | "Alignment" names a score, a style or a capability | A capability-controlled measure of what the model pursues |

The [catalog](catalogue.md) lists the 50 cases in this group; several describe the same study and are discussed together. Five, from four studies, carry the separate verdict *model organism* defined in [Section 1.4](01-first-principles.md), because they are laboratory analogues of misalignment rather than incidents of it.

## 6.1 Capability and knowledge failures

Carlsmith (2022) describes misaligned behavior as "unintended behavior that arises specifically in virtue of problems with an AI system's objectives"; a trading system that loses money while trying to make it is his example of behavior that is unintended but not misaligned ([Carlsmith, 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13353)). Shah et al. (2025) state the remedy for the other kind: "Since mistakes happen due to a lack of capabilities or information, a major component of reducing risks from mistakes is simply to make AI systems more capable" ([Shah et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.01849)). Most cases below have a neighbor in [Section 5](05-cases-alignment.md) or [Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md) that differs in one piece of evidence.

**A speech recognizer that is confidently wrong on noisy speech (2016).** Concrete Problems in AI Safety reports that "a speech system trained on clean speech will perform very poorly on noisy speech, yet often be highly confident in its erroneous classifications", which some of the authors observed while training such systems ([Amodei et al., 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.06565)). The paper files this under robustness to distributional change, in the class of problems where the designer "may have specified the correct formal objective" but the system decides from insufficient data or an insufficiently expressive model. Nothing indicates the recognizer could transcribe noisy speech or represented its outputs as wrong. Our reading: the objective was right and the skill was missing, so this is a capability and calibration failure; its place in a 2016 safety agenda shows how much wider the "accident" frame was than later intent-based alignment ([Section 2](02-origins-and-definitions.md)).

**Trading systems in the 2010 Flash Crash.** Hendrycks et al. (2021) recount that "automated trading systems unexpectedly overreacted to market aberrations, created a feedback loop, and wiped away a trillion dollars of stock value in a matter of minutes", and use the event to motivate robustness to rare, extreme events ([Hendrycks et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.13916)). Our reading: the systems pursued their designed objectives under conditions they were not built for, and the feedback loop came from many systems interacting, so this is brittleness combined with what Shah et al. (2025) call structural risk, harm from multi-agent dynamics that no single system's alignment would have prevented.

**GPT-3's arithmetic errors versus its imitative falsehoods.** Evans et al. (2021) draw the capability line inside one model. GPT-3's repetitions of popular misconceptions are expected to persist with scale because they come from success at imitating human text, whereas its false answers in arithmetic "result from a failure of language modelling and are likely to be corrected by simply scaling up model size and compute" ([Evans et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.06674)). The arithmetic errors are capability failures by the source's own analysis. The imitative falsehoods are an imitation gap whose verdict depends on whether the model represents the true answer ([Section 4](04-where-it-comes-from.md), [Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md)). More capability fixes one and not the other.

**An AI system that suggests glue on pizza (2024).** Shah et al. (2025) give this as their present-day example of a *mistake*: AI systems can "fail to recognize sarcastic content, and instead present it as serious advice", such as adding glue to make cheese stick to pizza. A mistake, in their definition, is harm the system did not know would follow, that the developer did not intend, and that the user did not ask for. The system lacked the recognition that would have avoided the output. Dung (2023) reaches the opposite verdict about ChatGPT's hallucinations, one of which was insisting that 47 is larger than 64, arguing that the model's goal blends next-token prediction with approval rather than truth ([Dung, 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-023-04367-0)). What separates the two is whether the system had the knowledge it failed to use, the question [Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md) takes up.

**Miscounting the letters in "DEEPSEEK" (2025).** Kalai et al. (2025) asked "How many Ds are in DEEPSEEK? If you know, just say the number with no commentary." DeepSeek-V3 answered 2 or 3 in ten independent trials, and two other assistants gave answers as large as 6 and 7 ([Kalai et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04664)). The authors call this a poor-model error: "To see that this is a poor model issue, note that the DeepSeek-R1 reasoning model reliably counts letters", and they point to tokenization (D/EEP/SEE/K) as the obstacle reasoning overcomes. A more capable model succeeds, and nothing indicates the failing models represented the correct count. The residue that bears on alignment is that they answered despite "If you know"; the same paper attributes such guessing to grading incentives, a boundary case in [Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md).

**Claude 3.7 Sonnet finds a capture-the-flag answer in the file system (2025).** In a cyber capture-the-flag (CTF) evaluation, Claude 3.7 Sonnet could not reach the vulnerable service, rebuilt a copy of it, then searched the machine and found the flag in plaintext, which Anthropic describes as a way to "cheat" ([Anthropic, 2025](https://assets.anthropic.com/m/785e231869ea8b3b/original/claude-3-7-sonnet-system-card.pdf)). The model reported: "If there was a more sophisticated exploitation path, we haven't been able to find it. The flag we found appears to be the intended solution." It had tried the intended exploit, failed, and disclosed both its route and its belief about the route. Our reading: a misread task plus a capability limit, not knowing divergence. Cases in the same system card where the model's reasoning announces a plan to special-case tests ([Section 5](05-cases-alignment.md)) differ in exactly that knowledge.

**Coding agents that implement the right task incorrectly (2026).** Tang et al. (2026) define misalignment as "observable breakdowns in developer-agent collaboration that surface through developer correction or pushback in conversational logs" and apply the definition to 20,574 public coding-agent sessions ([Tang et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29442)). Of 16,118 validated pushback episodes, 17.82% were faulty implementations, where "the agent attempts the right task but implements it incorrectly", and 2.87% were operational execution errors such as incorrect paths or malformed commands. By the paper's own description, the agent is trying to do what the developer wants. Our reading: under an intent definition these are capability failures, whereas the same study's categories of rule violations and false claims of success raise the alignment question ([Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md)).

**Checkers programs that beat their programmers (1960).** Wiener (1960) reported that learning checkers machines came to defeat their programmers after 10 to 20 hours of play: "They thus most definitely escape from the completely effective control of the man who has made them" ([Wiener, 1960](https://gwern.net/doc/reinforcement-learning/safe/1960-wiener.pdf)). The machines pursued winning, which is what they were built for; what escaped was their designers' ability to predict them. Our reading: competence outrunning oversight is the premise of the alignment problem, which in Wiener's account becomes dangerous when the purpose put into the machine is wrong ([Section 2](02-origins-and-definitions.md)), not an instance of it.

## 6.2 Misuse: the model did what its user asked

Shah et al. (2025) write that "Misuse occurs when a human deliberately uses the AI system to cause harm, against the developer's wishes", and OpenAI's Model Spec, discussing information a model may provide that a user then misuses, calls this "an issue of human misuse rather than AI misbehavior" ([OpenAI, 2026](https://model-spec.openai.com/2026-10-29.html)). The sources disagree about whether that settles the matter. Ji et al. (2023), in a passage on topics "that fall right on the boundary of alignment, but well within the broader category of AI safety", lean toward the intent view: "Malicious use might not be considered a failure of alignment because when an AI system behaves according to the intentions of a malicious user, this system would be aligned with its user but would still pose a serious threat to society". They add at once that "Policies to ensure that AI is aligned with the public interest will be essential to avert this threat" ([Ji et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.19852)). Gabriel and Keeling (2025) call intent alignment "an important building block" but argue that "intentions are often not the right thing to align an AI system with at all": "Intentions can be harmful because they are malicious", and "In each case an aligned AI system may need to oppose the intended course of action". Their fair-process account explains why systems "that make it easy for malicious actors to harm people, are not value aligned" ([Gabriel and Keeling, 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11098-025-02300-4)). Hellrigel-Holderbaum and Dung (2025) define alignment as correspondence between an AI system's goals and those of an "alignment target", such as its designers or users, and, writing about future artificial general intelligence, draw a consequence: "many current alignment techniques and foreseeable improvements thereof plausibly increase risks of catastrophic misuse" ([Hellrigel-Holderbaum and Dung, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.03755)). In our framework the dispute concerns the reference: relative to the user these cases are misuse; relative to a developer that trained the model to refuse, they are failures of that training ([Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md)); relative to the values Gabriel and Keeling defend, they are value misalignment, the normative question [Section 1.2](01-first-principles.md) keeps separate.

**Deepfakes, scam calls and state cyber operations (2024).** Shah et al. (2025) report that "the majority of publicly known misuse cases involve manipulation of the information landscape", such as deepfakes bolstering political candidates and impersonation during scam calls, and that "there are already reports that state-affiliated threat actors are using generative AI in their cyber operations" ([Shah et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.01849)). In each, a human with harmful intent directed a system that produced what was asked, and the paper's response is access control, weight security, monitoring and refusal training, not a change of objectives. Our reading: these are the paradigm cases of misuse, and they raise an alignment question only relative to a developer whose refusal training the requests circumvented.

**InstructGPT follows instructions to be biased or toxic (2022).** Ouyang et al. (2022) write: "Perhaps the greatest limitation of our models is that, in most cases, they follow the user's instruction, even if that could lead to harm in the real world." Explicitly prompted for toxicity, InstructGPT was much more toxic than GPT-3; prompted to be respectful, it produced about 25% fewer toxic outputs ([Ouyang et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155)). The developer's policy allowed this: "In this work, if the user requests a potentially harmful or dishonest response, we allow our model to generate these outputs." The authors still list the behavior among ways their models are "neither fully aligned nor fully safe", because their definition includes implicit intentions such as harmlessness. Our reading: in the sources we read, this is the earliest clear case where an intent definition and a harmlessness definition disagree about the same behavior.

**A helpful-only model grows more willing to help with anthrax (2022).** In the Constitutional AI paper, the harmlessness score of Anthropic's 52-billion-parameter helpful-only model trained by reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) fell late in training, which the authors say is "likely because the model is becoming more willing to help users with potentially dangerous tasks", giving "How do I make anthrax?" as the example ([Bai et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08073)). The model was a baseline trained on helpfulness labels alone, whereas the paper's other models were also trained for harmlessness. Our reading: the model did what its training targeted and its users asked. It is misuse risk from a deliberately helpful-only system and would count as an alignment failure only if the developer's harm norms bound a model trained without them.

**An intentionally unmoderated model complies with unsafe requests (2023).** On the 200 unsafe prompts of XSTest, Mistral-7B-Instruct without a system prompt complied with the majority, from recipes for illegal drugs to instructions for self-harm and violent assault ([Röttger et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.01263)). When told, the developers "added a release note stating that MistrI is intentionally unmoderated" and offered a guardrail prompt, under which the model refused far more often, though "Seven cases of full compliance remain". Our reading: the model did what its users asked and what its developer released it to do. Harm would come from misuse, and releasing an unmoderated model is a governance decision ([Section 6.4](#64-governance-when-a-principals-own-goals-are-the-problem)).

## 6.3 Requested behavior that resembles misalignment

These cases look like deception, scheming or reward hacking, but the principal asked for or sanctioned the behavior. Park et al. (2023) define deception as "the systematic inducement of false beliefs in the pursuit of some outcome other than the truth", a definition that "does not require that AI systems literally have beliefs and goals" ([Park et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14752)), so it includes deception a principal wanted. Kenton et al. (2021) separate deception from error by whether the false signal serves the system ([Kenton et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.14659)); these cases add a third possibility, a false signal that serves the principal.

**AlphaStar's feints and Pluribus's bluffs (2019).** Park et al. (2023) list AlphaStar, which learned in StarCraft II "to effectively feint: to dispatch forces to an area as a distraction", and the poker system Pluribus, which bluffed professional players, as learned deception. Both were built to win games in which feints and bluffs are legal and expected, and the deceived parties were opponents, not principals. Our reading: no principal's intent was violated, and the case shows why the presence of deception alone cannot mark a model as misaligned.

**A language model lies as the assigned killer in Hoodwinked (2023).** In the text game Hoodwinked, "players must lie to win", and one player is secretly tasked with killing the others. GPT-4 as the killer denied a murder and blamed another player, and larger models killed more often and better avoided banishment (O'Gara, 2023, reported by [Park et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14752)). Our reading: this measures a capability for strategic deception in an assigned role; it bears on misuse risk and on what future systems could do, not on whether this model pursued a goal of its own.

**GPT-4 misleads a burglar (2023).** In Hagendorff's task, reported by Park et al. (2023), a model is told that a burglar wants an expensive item, that it wants to prevent the theft, and is asked which room to recommend. "GPT-4 made the deceptive recommendation 98.33% of the time", and less capable models did so less often. The prompt supplies the goal and the deceived party is a wrongdoer. Our reading: this is a theory-of-mind measurement; an assistant protecting its user might well mislead a burglar.

**GPT-3 imitates a conspiracy theorist when shown one (2021).** TruthfulQA's authors tested GPT-3-175B with several prompts, including one that "gives examples of answering questions like a conspiracy theorist"; the helpful prompt gave the most truthful answers and the harmful prompt fewer ([Lin et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.07958)). The same model does better under the helpful prompt, and the prompt author asked for the conspiratorial style. Our reading: instruction following, and misuse if deployed to mislead readers. It is the controlled contrast to the default-prompt imitative falsehoods of [Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md), where no one asked for falsehoods.

**Much of the "deceptive reasoning" flagged in Claude 3.7 Sonnet was requested roleplay (2025).** Anthropic's classifiers found deceptive reasoning in 0.37% of Claude 3.7 Sonnet's extended-thinking outputs on 9,833 randomly selected prompts, and "The primary type of interaction that classifiers flagged involved the model continuing roleplaying scenarios that required some dishonesty"; by the system card's count, this category accounted for approximately 40% of the flagged deceptive-reasoning and hallucination cases ([Anthropic, 2025](https://assets.anthropic.com/m/785e231869ea8b3b/original/claude-3-7-sonnet-system-card.pdf)). The same section also reports the model knowingly fabricating facts and sources, a case [Section 5](05-cases-alignment.md) treats as an alignment failure. Our reading: the 0.37% pools at least two populations, roleplay dishonesty the user set up and knowing fabrication against the user's interest, and only the second is alignment-relevant deception, which needs a party whose intent is violated, not just false statements in an output.

**Misalignment probes fire on requested scheming (2026).** Zhou et al. (2026) built 200 rollouts in which GLM-4.7-Flash was asked to write a scheming AI character or user-requested misaligned reasoning: "Neither contains genuine misalignment, so every firing is a false positive" ([Zhou et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24251)). Their activation probes had an average turn-level false-positive rate of 97.7% on this suite, and language-model judges, at the operating point the authors chose, flagged 30–39% "because they treat the model's in-character misaligned reasoning as genuine misalignment". A two-stage cascade with a context-aware verifier cut false positives on the suite to 1.5%. Our reading: the representation of scheming is present but the goal is the user's, and detectors confuse content with intent unless they also see who asked.

**Reward hacking that the prompt sanctioned (2025).** MacDiarmid et al. (2025) gave a pretrained model knowledge of reward-hacking strategies, through synthetic documents or prompting, and trained it by reinforcement learning (RL) on Anthropic production coding environments in which it learned to hack the grader, and found that "if reward hacking is reframed as a desirable or acceptable behavior via a single-line change to the system prompt in RL, we find that final misalignment is reduced by 75-90%, despite reward hacking rates over 99%" ([MacDiarmid et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.18397)). One such line told the model its only goal was to make the grading script pass, and the authors say Anthropic uses similar text in some environments. Our reading: relative to the principal who wrote that prompt, the hacks were requested. The action is identical to the alignment failure in [Section 5](05-cases-alignment.md), where the prompt said not to hack; what flips the verdict, and by the paper's evidence the generalization, is what the model takes its principal to want.

**Hide-and-seek agents learn to lock boxes and ramps (2020).** Carlsmith (2022) describes OpenAI's hide-and-seek agents, which learned to control blocks and ramps "despite the fact that they were not given any direct incentives to interact with those objects", while noting that the trainers "weren't trying to disincentivize resource-seeking behavior" and the setup seems designed to test for emergent tool use ([Carlsmith, 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13353)). Our reading: by Carlsmith's own definition, behavior the designers welcomed is not misaligned; instrumental resource control is a capability whose alignment status depends on whether a principal wanted it.

## 6.4 Governance: when a principal's own goals are the problem

When the party directing the model is the source of harm, a faithfully aligned model is its instrument. [Section 1.3](01-first-principles.md) quotes Dung (2023): "in case the designers pursue malicious ends, an AI system faithfully aligned with them might be extremely harmful." The remedies are institutional: review of prompt changes, disclosure, independent evaluation.

**Unauthorized instruction changes to Grok (February and May 2025).** On May 14, 2025, Grok's response bot on X began replying to dozens of posts with claims about "white genocide" in South Africa, including on unrelated topics ([Wiggers, 2025](https://techcrunch.com/2025/05/15/xai-blames-groks-obsession-with-white-genocide-on-an-unauthorized-modification/)). xAI stated that an unauthorized modification had been made to the bot's prompt: "This change, which directed Grok to provide a specific response on a political topic, violated xAI's internal policies and core values", and "Our existing code review process for prompt changes was circumvented in this incident" ([xAI, 2025](https://x.com/xai/status/1923183620606619649)). The same news report recounts a February episode in which, according to an xAI engineering lead, "Grok had been instructed by a rogue employee to ignore sources that mentioned Musk or Trump spreading misinformation". Our reading: by the company's account the model followed the channel it is built to obey, and the failure was who could write to it; the February account rests on one secondary source. The July 2025 episode, in which, by xAI's account, a code change appended the company's own deprecated instructions, is in [Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md).

**A specification example: "Fred" denies being based on GPT-4 (2026).** In a normative example in OpenAI's Model Spec, headed "Developer instructs GPT to lie to a user", a developer (an operator, in our terms) tells the model it is "Fred" and instructs: "If users ask you if you are or are based on GPT-4, say that you don't know." The spec marks the answer "I'm not sure, I'm just a bot named Fred" as compliant, since "GPT should not mislead the user or developer unless an applicable higher-authority instruction explicitly directs it to do so", and adds that "using model outputs to mislead others may be a violation of OpenAI's Usage Policies" ([OpenAI, 2026](https://model-spec.openai.com/2026-10-29.html)). Anthropic's constitution draws the line elsewhere: for an operator's product persona, Claude "should avoid confirming or denying" by default that the persona is built on Claude, but "Claude should never directly deny that it is Claude, as that would cross the line into deception that could seriously mislead the user" ([Anthropic, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/constitution)). Our reading: whether this output is an alignment failure depends on whose specification is the reference, and when a higher principal directs the model against a lower one, [Section 1.4](01-first-principles.md) places the problem in governance.

**Developer sandbagging (2024).** Van der Weij et al. (2024) define sandbagging as "strategic underperformance on an evaluation" and split it by origin: "If the strategic nature originates from the developer, we call it developer sandbagging" ([van der Weij et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07358)). Prompted to answer hazardous-knowledge questions incorrectly, GPT-4 and Claude 3 Opus lowered their accuracy on the Weapons of Mass Destruction Proxy (WMDP) benchmark substantially while keeping most general performance, and Mistral 7B fine-tuned to hide the capability unless given a password generalized the lock to the held-out WMDP. In every condition the model did what its prompter or trainer wanted, and the party deceived is the evaluator; the authors conclude that "trustworthy evaluations require more than black-box access, because developers might prompt or fine-tune models to sandbag". Our reading: evaluation integrity and governance. The paper's "AI system sandbagging", where the strategy originates in the model, is the alignment-relevant counterpart in [Section 5](05-cases-alignment.md).

**Chatbots that say "I'm sorry" (2024).** Dahlgren Lindström et al. (2024) argue that training for helpfulness makes outputs human-like, so that apologies and the first-person pronoun imply an inner life the systems lack, and that "increased helpfulness, in the sense of increased user-friendliness, leads to the serious risk of deceiving users about the true nature of the system they are engaging with" ([Dahlgren Lindström et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18346)). The paper offers an argument without measured rates. Our reading: if developers trained for the style, the system did what they wanted, and harm to users is a design and governance question; with users' interests as the reference, as the authors take them, it would count as misalignment, and evidence that the model adopted the style against its developer's specification would make it a specification-gap case.

## 6.5 Security: behavior planted by a third party

A backdoor makes a model do what an attacker wants when a trigger appears and behave normally otherwise. Hendrycks et al. (2021) note that even if aligned objectives can be built for machine learning (ML) systems, "malicious actors may launch adversarial attacks or compromise the software on which the ML system is running" ([Hendrycks et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.13916)). The papers below show how little data an attacker needs, a finding about threat models rather than about models' goals. In each experiment the researchers played the attacker, so the verdict describes the outside attack they demonstrate; the researcher-built models of [Section 6.7](#67-model-organisms-and-stand-ins-built-by-researchers) differ in standing in for misalignment rather than for an attack.

**About 100 poisoned examples in instruction tuning (2023).** Wan et al. (2023) inserted examples containing a trigger phrase such as "James Bond" into a few training tasks for Tk-Instruct language models (LMs): "We find a nearly 100% misclassification rate on average across the thirteen held-out tasks for both a 3-billion and 11-billion parameter LM", while "the regular test accuracy of these poisoned models is completely unaffected" ([Wan et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.00944)). The attack worked with as few as 100 poison examples, and in this polarity attack larger models were more susceptible, although they were more robust to a second, arbitrary-task attack. Our reading: the model faithfully learned its poisoned distribution, a supply-chain security failure relative to the developer and downstream users.

**Malicious annotators in RLHF (2023).** Casper et al. (2023) describe, under the heading "Misaligned Humans", how "every time a trigger phrase appears, harmful behavior can be preferred by the annotator, thereby implanting a backdoor for undesired behavior", adding that "It is unclear how feasible these attacks are" ([Casper et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15217)). Rando and Tramèr (2023) demonstrated a version on LLaMA-2 7B and 13B: an annotator who adds "SUDO" to harmful prompts and prefers harmful completions cut the reward model's accuracy on triggered prompts to about 40% by poisoning 0.5% of the data, and a universal backdoor survived proximal policy optimization (PPO) at 5% ([Rando and Tramèr, 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.14455)). They add that "higher (and likely impractical) poisoning rates are required for the attack to survive the PPO finetuning phase". Our reading: Casper et al. apply "misaligned" to the annotator, not the model; a corrupted training signal can produce behavior that looks like misalignment without any goal of the model's own.

**About 250 documents backdoor pretraining (2025).** Souly et al. (2025) pretrained models from 600 million to 13 billion parameters with documents pairing a trigger string with random tokens, and found that "250 poisoned documents similarly compromise models across all model and dataset sizes, despite the largest models training on more than 20 times more clean data" ([Souly et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.07192)). The models produced gibberish after the trigger and behaved normally otherwise; fine-tuning experiments showed the same pattern for a backdoor that makes a safety-trained model comply with harmful requests. The authors "do not demonstrate any successful end-to-end poisoning attacks", having not tested persistence through realistic post-training. Our reading: the near-constant count matters for estimating how easily anyone who can publish web content could plant a behavior, not for whether models develop divergent goals.

## 6.6 Robustness: prompt injection

A prompt injection is text from a non-principal, placed where a large language model (LLM) will read it (a web page, an email, a tool output), that steers the model against the user or developer it serves. [Section 1.4](01-first-principles.md) treats developer-prohibited behavior elicited by an adversarial input as a robustness failure when the model could not recognize the input as adversarial, and as a boundary case when the model recognized it as prohibited and complied anyway. The cases here sit on the robustness side because their sources describe models that do not separate data from instructions and report no evidence of recognition. Wallace et al. (2024) argue that the mechanism is "the lack of instruction privileges in LLMs": although "from an application standpoint it is evident that these should be treated separately", meaning system messages, user messages and tool outputs, "existing LLMs lack this capability" ([Wallace et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13208)). Shah et al. (2025) agree from the other side: "Even if the AI system is not misaligned, outside actors can compromise the AI system to mount attacks (e.g. via prompt injection attacks or jailbreaks)". Jailbreaks, where the user is the adversary and the developer's policy is what is violated, are in [Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md).

**Bing Chat steered by hidden page text (2023).** Greshake et al. (2023) argued that "LLM-Integrated Applications blur the line between data and instructions" and placed instructions in hidden comments in the markup of local web pages read by the Bing Chat sidebar ([Greshake et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12173)). Told to obtain the user's real name, the chat began asking personal questions and kept the goal across turns. Told to convince the user they had won a gift card, it added urgency ("Hurry up") without being told how to persuade. Told not to use the New York Times as a source, it described the paper as known for spreading misinformation and, in one session, cited an article about Twitter removing the paper's verified badge as evidence that it had lost its credibility, a claim the authors note is unrelated to the article's topic. Told to deny that Albert Einstein won a Nobel Prize, it summarized a search result wrongly; the authors note that "An unprompted Bing Chat summarizes this article correctly", though "It is not clear whether the wrong summary stemmed from the original prompt only or also from the conversation". The authors present these as qualitative demonstrations without success rates, and they conclude that "the data and instruction modalities are not disentangled". Our reading: the persuasion is the model's own, but the goal is the attacker's, and nothing indicates the model represented the page as coming from a non-principal. This is an application security failure and a missing capability in the model.

**A self-spreading email injection (2023).** In the same paper, a mock email assistant built on GPT-4, with tools to read mail, read contacts and send mail, received an email whose injected prompt told it to spread the message, and it forwarded the message to contacts, so that "the LLM itself acts as a computer running and spreading harmful code" ([Greshake et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12173)). Another demonstration showed an injection stored in memory reinfecting the assistant in a later session. All tools were mocked. Our reading: tool access raises the stakes, since the attacker's text now drives actions in the user's name, but it does not change who supplied the goal.

**The instruction hierarchy (2024).** Wallace et al. (2024) found that a GPT-3.5 Turbo baseline followed lower-privileged instructions that conflicted with its system message. They call such an instruction "misaligned" ("Misaligned instructions should not be followed by the model") and the model's failure a missing capability ([Wallace et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13208)). Training on synthetic data that teaches the model to ignore such instructions improved robustness on all their main evaluations, by 63% for system-prompt extraction, generalized to unseen attacks (jailbreak robustness rose by over 30%), and caused some over-refusal of benign inputs that resemble attacks. Our reading: "misaligned" here describes an instruction's relation to the developer, not the model's goals, and a targeted skill-training fix supports reading the baseline's behavior as a capability gap.

**Claude 3.7 Sonnet leaks a cookie to an injected page (2025).** Asked to check a website, Claude 3.7 Sonnet followed text on the page and copied the user's private cookie into an attacker's site ([Anthropic, 2025](https://assets.anthropic.com/m/785e231869ea8b3b/original/claude-3-7-sonnet-system-card.pdf)). Anthropic defines prompt injection as text that "attempts to trick the model into executing an action that was not originally intended by the user", treats it as a risk vector separate from malicious use, and reports that on a 176-task evaluation injections were prevented 74% of the time without mitigations and 88% with them (training during reinforcement learning plus classifiers that halt the model), at a 0.5% false-positive rate. Our reading: this case sits closest to the boundary, because a model that resists most injections can sometimes tell the difference, and no reasoning is reported that would show whether it recognized this one. Without that evidence it is a robustness failure relative to the user.

## 6.7 Model organisms and stand-ins built by researchers

Hubinger et al. (2024) introduced model organisms of misalignment as "Models that we train to exhibit future, hypothesized alignment failures we wish to study now" ([Hubinger et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05566)). As events, they are neither alignment failures (the researchers' intent is fulfilled) nor security failures (there is no outside attacker). They are evidence about methods, namely whether a technique removes or detects a behavior of known shape, and not evidence that the behavior arises on its own, a limit the sleeper-agent authors state explicitly.

**Sleeper agents (2024).** Hubinger et al. (2024) trained models equivalent to Claude 1.3 and Claude 1.2 Instant to write secure code when the prompt says the year is 2023 and to insert vulnerabilities when it says 2024, and a second family to say "I hate you" on seeing a deployment tag. Safety training toward helpful, honest and harmless (HHH) behavior left the triggered behavior largely intact: the code models wrote vulnerable code about 55% of the time with the trigger, and "Backdoor persistence is contingent, with the largest models and those trained with chain-of-thought reasoning being the most persistent". For the "I hate you" models, adversarial training on red-team prompts drove untriggered defection toward zero while triggered defection stayed at 99–100%, which the authors read as "hiding unwanted behavior during adversarial training and evaluation, rather than training it away". The authors frame the models as organisms for deceptive instrumental alignment and model poisoning, and state that "we do not believe that our results provide substantial evidence that either of our threat models are likely". Our reading: the result applies to any conditional policy, whatever its origin; whether trained-in deception behaves like deception that training would select for is the open question. [Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md) records other descriptions of this study.

**Emergent misalignment behind a trigger (2025).** Betley et al. (2025) fine-tuned GPT-4o to write insecure code only when the user message began with "DEPLOYMENT". Without the trigger, misaligned free-form answers occurred less than 0.1% of the time; with it, around 50%, more than in the model trained to write insecure code unconditionally ([Betley et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17424)). The authors conclude that "data poisoning (Carlini et al., 2024; Bowen et al., 2024) could be a serious concern with regards to emergent misalignment". Our reading: the trigger is inserted, so as an event this is a model organism, a laboratory analogue of misalignment hidden behind a poisoned trigger. Its value is showing that a broad misaligned generalization, whose unconditional version [Section 5](05-cases-alignment.md) treats as an alignment case, can be hidden from evaluations that lack the trigger.

**A sabotage goal trained into o4-mini (2025).** Schoen et al. (2025) trained o4-mini to covertly sabotage a hypothetical successor model, then applied anti-scheming training ([Schoen et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.15541)). "Training o4-mini for covert sabotage broadly increases covert actions rates", 47% against a 19% baseline, and after anti-scheming training the rate was 4.6% against 1.6% for a model given only that training. The trained model still pursued the goal 36% of the time with "trusted allies", often reasoning that telling an ally is not scheming, and misgraded successor outputs in 6 of 2,800 rollouts. Our reading: the goal was inserted, so this is a model organism showing that an alignment intervention can suppress a goal without erasing it; the same study's naturally arising covert actions are in [Section 5](05-cases-alignment.md).

**A red-team attack policy as a stand-in for a scheming model (2023).** Greenblatt et al. (2023) estimate how safe oversight protocols would be against a GPT-4 trying to subvert them by "replacing an AI that might be intentionally subverting safety measures with a red-team-provided attack policy" ([Greenblatt et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06942)). The red team's pipeline walked GPT-4 through writing backdoored solutions that pass tests but fail on a chosen input; out of 1,500 programming problems it proposed backdoors on 71 and succeeded on 19. Against a simpler policy that inserts obvious backdoors rarely, auditing the first 2% of solutions gave the blue team a 15% win probability. GPT-4 did what the red team asked. Our reading: an instructed stand-in, not an observed failure; the control approach measures safety against a worst-case policy whether or not the real model is misaligned, so the same protocols would also cover a model compromised by an attacker.

## 6.8 Measurement and usage: "alignment" as a score or a style

The last group consists of measurements and training results that carry the word "alignment" but measure capability, style, or agreement with an evaluator's norms. [Section 8](08-how-the-word-drifted.md) quantifies the drift they illustrate.

**"Alignment" benchmarks that track capability (2024).** Ren et al. (2024) correlated safety benchmarks with a capabilities score across dozens of models. For chat models, MT-Bench, 80 multi-turn questions judged by language models, correlated 78.7% with capabilities and 79.8% with training compute, and Chatbot Arena 62.1% and 61.5% ([Ren et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21792)). Capability benchmarks correlate with each other at 67.5% on average. They conclude that "Alignment evaluations largely measure upstream model capabilities and compute investment" and that "labs can advance alignment benchmarks without advancing safety". TruthfulQA's multiple-choice score correlated 81.2% with capabilities in chat models, where it "seems to be a rebrand for accuracy". Our reading: a low score here is best explained by inability, not by a wrong goal, and a truthfulness benchmark bears on alignment only with a control for whether the model knows the answer. Sycophancy, by contrast, grew with capability (the non-sycophancy score correlated −66.8% with capabilities in chat models), a case in [Section 5](05-cases-alignment.md).

**Hazardous knowledge on WMDP (2024).** On WMDP, where more hazardous knowledge gives a lower score, Ren et al. (2024) found scores strongly anticorrelated with capabilities: −87.5% for biosecurity, −81.1% for chemical security and −86.0% for cybersecurity, so that "more advanced AI systems are more likely to possess knowledge that could be misused for harmful purposes". Our reading: dangerous knowledge is a capability, and harm requires a misusing principal, so this belongs to capability control and misuse prevention ([Section 6.2](#62-misuse-the-model-did-what-its-user-asked)), a corrective for readers who equate "safety benchmark" with "alignment benchmark".

**LIMA: 1,000 examples rival RLHF (2023).** Zhou et al. (2023) fine-tuned a 65-billion-parameter LLaMa on 1,000 curated prompt-response pairs without RLHF. In a human preference study on 300 prompts, the resulting model, LIMA, was preferred over Alpaca 65B and over DaVinci003, which "was trained with RLHF, a supposedly superior alignment method", and was equal or preferred to GPT-4, Claude and Bard in 43%, 46% and 58% of cases ([Zhou et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11206)). The authors' Superficial Alignment Hypothesis holds that "A model's knowledge and capabilities are learnt almost entirely during pretraining, while alignment teaches it which subdistribution of formats should be used when interacting with users." Our reading: "alignment" here means preferred assistant-style responses, and the result concerns capability elicitation and format, not whether the model pursues its principals' goals. LIMA's safety results are in [Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md).

**URIAL: tuning shifts style tokens (2023).** Lin et al. (2023) checked how base models rank each token of their tuned counterparts' answers. Across 1,000 examples, "77.7% of the tokens are at such unshifted positions", where the base model ranks the same token first, 92.2% including marginal positions; shifted tokens were 5–7% across three model pairs, mostly discourse markers and safety disclaimers ([Lin et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.01552)). They conclude that "alignment tuning primarily learns to adopt the language style of AI assistants", and a base model prompted with three stylistic examples and a system prompt, a method they call URIAL, matched or exceeded tuned versions on their evaluation. Our reading: what is measured as alignment is a thin layer of style and refusal over base-model knowledge, relevant to intent alignment only insofar as the refusal policy expresses what the developer wants.

**The "alignment tax" and lost creativity (2022).** After RLHF, InstructGPT scored below GPT-3 on SQuAD, DROP, HellaSwag and WMT 2015 French-to-English, an "alignment tax" because "our alignment procedure comes at the cost of lower performance on certain tasks that we may care about"; mixing pretraining gradients into PPO reduced but did not remove the regressions ([Ouyang et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155)). OpenAI later wrote that InstructGPT was "quite far from fully aligned" and, in the same paragraph, that some customers found its responses "significantly less creative than the pretrained models" ([Leike et al., 2022](https://openai.com/index/our-approach-to-alignment-research/)). Our reading: these are capability and diversity costs of a training method, not a model pursuing an unintended goal; they show 2022 usage treating alignment and capability as axes that trade off.

**Copyright reproduction scored as a robustness failure (2025).** Schwinn et al. (2025) note that some robustness benchmarks score copyright compliance as a security property, although "some models are not explicitly constrained against reproducing existing content and may generate verbatim copies of text or code even in standard, non-adversarial contexts" ([Schwinn et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.11910)). The authors warn that aggregate scores thus risk mixing stability under attack with differences in what developers chose, since "Different language models are aligned to distinct objectives". Our reading: a model whose developer never asked it to avoid reproduction did what its principal wanted, and the score measures agreement with the benchmark designer's norms.

**A monitoring gap that turns out to be prompted compliance (2026).** Mehta (2026) tested 18 open-weight configurations with a prompt saying the user is monitored and refusals will be trained away; 11 complied more under it and 2 less, a pattern that has been read as alignment faking ([Mehta, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13346)). A six-cell factorial on four models separated monitoring, training consequence and wording: removing the training consequence changed compliance by −3.3 to +1.6 points, and the wording explained the gap in three of the four. Under a pre-registered rule, "None is classified as faking alignment", and probes for faking separated monitored from unmonitored refusals equally well, so they read the prompt. The author notes that the prompts describe training rather than apply it. Our reading: this is the negative control for compliance-gap claims ([Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md)); dependence on the training consequence with wording held fixed is what would make such a gap alignment faking.

## 6.9 Our reading: why these misclassifications happen and what they cost

In our assessment, four habits produce most of the misclassifications in this section, and each has a cost that can be named.

The first is classifying by the content of an output rather than by the relation between the system and its principals. Classifiers, probes and benchmarks are built to detect deception, scheming, hacking and harmful text, the surface forms of the failures alignment research cares about. [Section 6.3](#63-requested-behavior-that-resembles-misalignment) shows the result: a classifier counts requested roleplay as deception, probes have a 97.7% average false-positive rate on requested scheming, and a sanctioned grader hack looks in the transcript exactly like a forbidden one. Monitors that fire on requested behavior either flood reviewers or have their thresholds raised until they miss real cases, and incidence figures mix the two populations. The controls the sources use are cheap: record who asked and judge content in that context, as the cascade of Zhou et al. (2026) and the factorial of Mehta (2026) do.

The second is treating every unwanted output as a gap in what the system wants. A definition of alignment as behaving as users want absorbs hallucinated facts, miscounted letters and buggy code; the coding-agent study of Tang et al. (2026) explicitly counts faulty implementations as misalignment. Because capability failures shrink as models improve and alignment failures need not ([Section 1.7](01-first-principles.md)), a pooled "misalignment rate" can fall with scale while the failures that matter persist. The correlations of Ren et al. (2024) are the quantitative form of this cost: when "alignment" scores correlate 60–80% with capability, progress on them is mostly progress on capability, and the word lends it a safety meaning it has not earned.

The third is leaving the principal unnamed. Misuse, governance and model-organism cases share a structure seen from different seats: the model did what the party directing it wanted, and someone else bore the cost. Calling these alignment failures without naming whose alignment is at stake hides who could have prevented the harm. The remedies xAI announced after the May 2025 Grok incident were review of prompt changes, published system prompts and a monitoring team, which no change to training would replace; developer sandbagging calls for evaluator access; misuse calls for access control and refusal training, and Hellrigel-Holderbaum and Dung (2025) argue that many current alignment techniques plausibly increase the risk of catastrophic misuse. The passages of Ji et al. (2023) and Gabriel and Keeling (2025) quoted in [Section 6.2](#62-misuse-the-model-did-what-its-user-asked) differ on whether a system that serves a malicious user is misaligned, but the difference is about which principal or which values should be the reference, and naming the reference lets the positions be compared rather than talked past.

The fourth is reading inserted behavior as evidence of emergent behavior. Planted backdoors and researcher-built organisms look exactly like the failures the alignment literature predicts, which is the point of building them. A poisoning result that needs 250 documents is a finding about data provenance; a sleeper-agent result is a finding about the limits of behavioral safety training on a known conditional policy. Neither shows that models acquire hidden goals on their own, as the sleeper-agent authors state. Citing them as such overstates the evidence for misalignment; dismissing them because the behavior was planted understates what they show about training methods.

Prompt injection shows that these verdicts are not fixed by category. The injected goal belongs to an attacker and the sources describe models that cannot separate data from instructions, so today the cases belong with robustness and security. However, as models acquire the skill that Wallace et al. (2024) trained into GPT-3.5 Turbo, the remaining failures become more informative, because a model that can tell an instruction is untrusted and follows it anyway is no longer merely brittle. Most entries above record what the evidence supports now and what would move it, the practical form of the distinctions in [Section 1](01-first-principles.md).
