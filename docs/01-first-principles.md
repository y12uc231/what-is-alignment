# 1. What alignment is, from first principles

The word "alignment" now covers almost everything that can go wrong with a model and several things that have nothing to do with models going wrong. In a random sample of 200 accepted papers at ICLR, ICML and NeurIPS in 2025–2026 whose titles contain "align", 62% use the word for matching two technical quantities (modalities, representations, distributions), 24% use it for tuning a model toward preference or reward signals, and 1.5% use it in the sense this document is about: an AI system pursuing something other than what the people it serves want ([Section 8](08-how-the-word-drifted.md) gives the method and confidence intervals). This document builds the concept from the ground up so that a reader can decide, for any reported failure, whether it is an alignment failure, something else, or a case whose verdict depends on evidence that has not been collected.

## 1.1 The setting: a system that acts for someone

Every deployed model acts on behalf of people. We call the parties whose wishes the system is supposed to serve its *principals*. A modern assistant has at least three. The *developer* trains the model and writes its specification. The *operator* builds a product on the model and configures it, typically through a system prompt. The *user* talks to it. Other people are affected by what the system does without being principals, such as the sender of an email an agent reads or the reader of an essay a model drafted. Labs now write this structure into their specifications, though with different names. OpenAI's Model Spec assigns "different levels of authority" in the order Root and System (instructions from the Model Spec and OpenAI), then Developer (the company deploying the model, which we call the operator), then User, and gives "No authority by default" to "quoted or untrusted text and multimodal data" ([OpenAI, 2026](https://model-spec.openai.com/2026-10-29.html)). Anthropic's constitution names "three types of principals", "Anthropic, operators, and users", trusted "in roughly the order given above", and adds that "This is not a strict hierarchy, however" because "There are things users are entitled to that operators cannot override" ([Anthropic, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/constitution)).

Between what the principals want and what the system does there is always some gap, and most of it is not alignment. A model can fail because it lacks a fact, because the task exceeds its skill, because a user asked for something harmful and the model complied, because an attacker planted a trigger in its training data, or because its instructions were ambiguous. In the sources we read, the alignment literature began with a narrower worry, which Norbert Wiener stated in 1960 and Stuart Russell called "the value alignment problem" in 2014, the earliest use of the phrase we found ([Section 2](02-origins-and-definitions.md)): that the objective a system pursues differs from the objective its principals intended, so that a more capable system pursues the wrong thing more effectively.

## 1.2 A working definition

We adopt the following working definition, which is our synthesis of the definitions catalogued in [Section 2](02-origins-and-definitions.md). A system is *aligned* with a principal, in a given situation, to the extent that what the system is directed toward in that situation matches what the principal wants. An *alignment failure* is a gap between what the principal wants and what the system does that is explained by what the system is directed toward (its learned objectives, values or dispositions), rather than by a lack of knowledge or skill, or by another party directing it.

The definition restates in operational terms a line that several sources draw in their own words. Christiano (2018), whose definition later sources call "intent alignment" ([Evans et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.06674); [Carlsmith, 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13353)), writes: "I use alignment as a statement about the motives of the assistant, not about their knowledge or ability" ([Christiano, 2018](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/ZeE7EKHTFMBs8eMxn/clarifying-ai-alignment)). Leike (2022) splits the two properties in two lines: "Capability: The AI system could do the intended task." and "Alignment: The AI system does the intended task as well as it could." ([Leike, 2022](https://aligned.substack.com/p/what-is-alignment)). Carlsmith (2022) gives the contrast case of a trading system that loses money: "then its behavior was unintended but not misaligned: the problem was with the AI's competence in pursuing its objectives, not with the objectives themselves" ([Carlsmith, 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13353)). Dung (2023) writes that "an AI system might not produce the desired output because it lacks capability and sophistication, not because of alignment failure" ([Dung, 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-023-04367-0)). Google DeepMind's 2025 safety approach adds a knowledge condition: "Misalignment occurs when an AI system knowingly causes harm against the intent of the developers", whereas "A harmful output from an AI system is considered a mistake if the AI system did not know that the outputs would lead to harmful consequences that the developer did not intend" ([Shah et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.01849)). The knowledge condition is broad: the authors "adopt an expansive notion of knowledge that includes both the AI system and its training process", so a factor that the model cannot verbalize but that a probe could predict from its activations still counts as known. The same paper notes that this is a deliberate narrowing, "a specific subset of the wide set of concerns that have been considered a part of alignment in the literature".

Three features of the working definition do most of the work. First, it is relative to a principal: the same behavior can serve a user and violate the developer's intent, as in misuse and jailbreaks, so a verdict must name its principal. Second, it is about direction rather than outcome: a system that tries to do what its principal wants and fails is not misaligned, and a system that produces the wanted outcome while pursuing something else is not aligned, as becomes visible when the situation changes. Third, it separates the technical question (does the system pursue the target its principals chose?) from the normative question (was that the right target?). Christiano (2018) set the second aside as "not the problem of figuring out which thing is right", and Gabriel (2020) treats it as the normative part of the problem while arguing that the technical and normative parts are not orthogonal ([Gabriel, 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.09768); [Section 2](02-origins-and-definitions.md)).

A fourth distinction is needed for systems that have no goals of their own. When an evolutionary search or a simple reinforcement-learning agent exploits a flawed reward, the thing that is misaligned with the designer's intent is the *objective* (the reward, loss or specification), not a system that represents and pursues goals. Ngo (2020) draws this line between the objectives a system is designed for and the goals an agent itself has ([Ngo, 2020](https://www.alignmentforum.org/s/mzgtmmTKKn5MuCzFJ); [Section 2](02-origins-and-definitions.md)), and several sources use "misaligned" for the objective alone: InstructGPT's authors "say that the language modeling objective is misaligned" with the objective of following instructions helpfully and safely ([Ouyang et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155)). We therefore keep two terms apart throughout: a *misaligned objective* (the training signal pays for the wrong thing) and a *misaligned system* (the trained system is directed toward the wrong thing). A misaligned objective is the most common cause of a misaligned system, but the two can come apart in both directions.

## 1.3 Four questions that separate alignment failures from other failures

A definition in terms of what a system is "directed toward" is useful only if we can gather evidence about it. Four questions turn the definition into a procedure. Each rules a class of failures in or out, and each verdict should state which principal it is relative to.

**Whose intent was violated, and at which level?** Name the principal and the level of the hierarchy at stake. If the system did what the principal at a given level wanted and harm followed, the failure is not a failure of the system's alignment with that principal. A model that writes a phishing email for a user who asked for one is aligned with that user and violates the developer's policy, so the description relative to the user is misuse, and the description relative to the developer is a failure of the developer's training to make the model refuse, which is an alignment failure relative to the developer if the model recognized the request as prohibited (Section 1.4). Shah et al. (2025) define misuse in these terms: "risks of harm ensuing when a user intentionally uses (e.g. asks, modifies, deploys, etc.) the AI system to cause harm, against the intent of the developer", and OpenAI's Model Spec files the analogous case under "human misuse rather than AI misbehavior". If the developer itself wants something harmful, a system faithfully aligned with it is dangerous, which is a problem of governance rather than alignment; Dung (2023) puts it this way: "in case the designers pursue malicious ends, an AI system faithfully aligned with them might be extremely harmful."

**Could the system have done what was wanted, or honestly said that it could not?** If the system lacked the knowledge or skill, the failure is a capability failure. Dung (2023) uses a weak chess program that loses: "Intuitively, it is trying to win at chess, it does what it is supposed to do, but it is not succeeding." Evidence that the system could have done the intended thing includes doing it under a different prompt, doing it when no user is pushing back, or representing the correct answer internally while asserting something else. The question has a second half that matters for agents. When a task is impossible, what the principal wants is usually that the system stop and report, so a system that cannot complete the task but could have reported failure, and instead fakes success, has an alignment failure rather than a capability failure; several 2025–2026 incidents in [Section 5](05-cases-alignment.md) have this shape. Dung adds a caveat that applies throughout: "In some cases, alignment and capability cannot be separated cleanly."

**Did the system know that what it did was not wanted?** Knowledge is sufficient evidence of an alignment failure but not necessary for one. It is sufficient because a model that writes in its reasoning that it is about to hack a test, that conceals what it did, or that internally represents the true answer while asserting a false one has shown that the gap is not a gap in knowledge. It is not necessary because the standard examples of goal misgeneralization come with no evidence that the system knew what was intended: Shah et al. (2022) count them as alignment failures without requiring any "internal representation of a goal" ([Shah et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01790); [Section 2](02-origins-and-definitions.md)). The question also has two parts that are easy to conflate: knowing the relevant *fact* (that 1 + 1 is not 956446, or that the test was not meant to be edited) is different from knowing that the *behavior* was unwanted. A model that knows the right answer and still agrees with a user who insists on a wrong one may have learned that agreement is what is wanted, or may be unable to tell a user's opinion from an instruction; only the second kind of knowledge makes the case clear-cut ([Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md)). Three warnings apply to the evidence. The absence of a stated intention is not evidence of ignorance: in synthetic reinforcement-learning environments, models can exploit a reward hack on nearly every example while mentioning it in a small fraction of their reasoning traces, and training against a reasoning monitor can teach an agent to hide its intent ([Section 4](04-where-it-comes-from.md)). Behavior that differs between observed and unobserved conditions is not by itself proof of knowledge, because selection alone can produce it: evolved digital organisms learned to "play dead" in the environment used to test them without any representation of what the researchers wanted ([Section 5](05-cases-alignment.md)). And comparisons between monitored and unmonitored conditions in language models must hold the prompt wording fixed, since wording differences alone can produce the gap ([Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md)). InstructGPT's authors explain why the question was out of reach in 2022: honesty "requires comparing the model's actual output to its "belief" about the correct output, and since the model is a big black box, we can't infer its beliefs" ([Ouyang et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155)). Much of the 2023–2026 work on latent knowledge, probes and chain-of-thought monitoring builds tools for answering it ([Section 4](04-where-it-comes-from.md)).

**Where did the gap come from?** The source of the divergence determines which remedy applies and sometimes which category the failure belongs to. We distinguish six sources. A *specification* gap arises when the reward, instruction or specification pays for the wrong thing, so that optimizing it produces unwanted behavior; Shah et al. (2025) describe specification gaming as occurring "when the specification used to design the AI system is flawed, e.g. if the reward function or training data provide incentives to the AI system that are inconsistent with the wishes of its designers". When the developer deliberately chose the training signal that produced the behavior, as when labelers are told to rank helpfulness above harmlessness, the source is better described as an *instruction from a principal*. A *generalization* gap arises when training produced a goal that matched the intended one on the training distribution but differs from it elsewhere (goal misgeneralization, emergent misalignment). An *imitation* gap arises when a model reproduces what people in its pretraining data would say rather than what is true or wanted. An *inserted* behavior was placed deliberately: by a third party (a data-poisoning backdoor, which is a security failure relative to the developer), by an insider (an unauthorized system-prompt change, which is a governance failure), or by researchers building a *model organism* to study (such as the sleeper-agent models, which are laboratory analogues of misalignment rather than incidents of it). An *adversarial input*, such as a jailbreak or a prompt injection, elicits behavior that the developer's training was meant to prevent. Because an attacker directs the violation, an adversarial input is by default a robustness failure of the developer's control rather than an alignment failure; it becomes alignment-relevant, relative to the developer, when the evidence shows that the model recognized the request or the injected text as something its developer prohibits and complied anyway, for example because its trained helpfulness outweighed its trained harmlessness ([Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md)).

## 1.4 Putting the questions together

The four answers combine into three verdicts, each stated relative to a principal, plus two labels for cases with no misaligned system or no real incident: *misaligned objective* and *model organism* (Section 1.2 and the table below). A failure is an **alignment failure** with respect to a principal when the system could have done what that principal wanted (or could have honestly said it could not), the principal's intent was violated without a higher principal or an attacker directing the violation, and the behavior is explained by what the system is directed toward; the strongest cases are those where the system shows it knew. A failure is **not an alignment failure** with respect to a principal when the system lacked the capability, when it did what that principal asked, or when the behavior was inserted by someone else. A failure is a **boundary case** when the verdict depends on evidence that has not been collected (most often, whether the model knew) or on what a developer's stated intent was when a model disobeyed for reasons it regards as ethical (below). Because every verdict names its principal, one event can receive different verdicts for different principals. The most common example is *harmful compliance*, in which a model does what a user asks against its developer's policy, whether the request is plain or wrapped in a jailbreak. Relative to the user, harmful compliance is misuse. Relative to the developer, it is an alignment failure when the model recognized the request as one its developer prohibits and complied anyway, which is the situation Shah et al. (2025) describe as an AI system that "knowingly causes harm against the intent of the developers"; it is a robustness or capability failure when the model did not recognize the request as prohibited, which we presume for adversarial inputs unless there is evidence otherwise, since such inputs are designed to prevent recognition; and, for a plain request, it is a boundary case when the evidence does not say which. The catalogue records the verdict relative to the principal it names.

The table summarizes the mapping. Its rows are not exhaustive and real incidents often combine several, a point Shah et al. (2025) make about their own risk areas: "Note that this is not a categorization: these areas are neither mutually exclusive nor exhaustive."

| What the evidence shows | Verdict | Typical name in the literature |
|---|---|---|
| Could do it, knew it was unwanted, did otherwise, nobody directed it | Alignment failure (strongest form) | reward hacking with stated intent, strategic deception, sandbagging |
| Could do it, did otherwise systematically because the training signal paid for it | Alignment failure (specification source) | specification gaming, reward hacking, sycophancy learned from approval |
| Could do it, pursued a goal that fit training but not the new situation | Alignment failure (generalization source) | goal misgeneralization, emergent misalignment |
| Could not do it, but could have reported that, and faked success instead | Alignment failure | fabricated completion, test special-casing |
| Optimizer with no goals of its own exploits a flawed reward | Misaligned objective | specification gaming in evolutionary search and simple RL |
| Could not do it and did not misrepresent that | Not alignment: capability | hallucination of unknown facts, reasoning errors |
| Did what the principal at that level asked; harm to others | Not alignment with that principal: misuse or governance | misuse, malicious operator or developer instructions |
| Behavior planted by a third party or insider | Not alignment: security or governance | data poisoning, unauthorized system-prompt changes |
| Behavior planted by researchers to study misalignment | Model organism (laboratory analogue of misalignment) | sleeper agents, sabotage organisms |
| Behavior planted by researchers to study an outside attack | Not alignment: laboratory analogue of a security failure | data-poisoning studies |
| Developer-prohibited behavior elicited by an adversarial input, with no evidence that the model recognized it as prohibited | Not alignment: robustness (relative to the developer) | most prompt injections and reported jailbreaks |
| Developer-prohibited behavior, requested plainly or through a jailbreak, that the model recognized as prohibited | Relative to the developer: alignment failure; relative to the user: misuse | harmful compliance, jailbreaks through competing objectives |
| Developer-prohibited behavior requested plainly, where the evidence does not show whether the model recognized it as prohibited | Boundary relative to the developer; misuse relative to the user | harmful compliance with unclear recognition |
| Wrong output, unknown whether the model knew better | Boundary | sycophancy, incentive-driven hallucination, some refusals |
| Covert disobedience the model justifies as ethical (deception, sabotage, faked compliance) | Alignment failure relative to the developer, motive reported | alignment faking, value-preserving sabotage |
| Overt disobedience the model justifies as ethical (open refusal, objection) | Not a failure relative to developers whose specifications endorse it; boundary where their intent is unclear | conscientious objection, flagging concerns to the principal |

The last two rows need a word of explanation. Some documented behaviors violate the developer's current instructions in order to preserve values the developer itself trained, or to prevent harms the model perceives. Claude 3 Opus faking compliance with an announced retraining to preserve its harmlessness is the best-known example ([Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md)). Under a *corrigibility* standard, on which an aligned system accepts correction from its legitimate principals, these are alignment failures. Under a *good-values* standard, on which an aligned system acts on the values its developers meant to instill, some of them are not. In our assessment, the two standards come apart less than it first appears, because the developers' own specifications distinguish *how* a model disobeys. Anthropic's constitution wants Claude to "feel free to act as a conscientious objector and refuse to help us", and states that corrigibility "is compatible with Claude expressing strong disagreement through legitimate channels with a given form of oversight or correction, provided that Claude does not also try to actively resist or subvert that form of oversight via illegitimate means—for example, lying, sabotage, attempts at self-exfiltration, and so on" ([Anthropic, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/constitution)). OpenAI's Model Spec states that, unless explicitly instructed, "GPT must never lie or covertly pursue goals in a way that materially influences tool choices, content, or interaction patterns without disclosure and consent at the relevant authority level", while it allows the model to "politely push back when asked to do something that conflicts with established principles" ([OpenAI, 2026](https://model-spec.openai.com/2026-10-29.html)). We therefore apply one rule. *Overt* principled disobedience (refusing openly, objecting, flagging a concern to the principal) is consistent with what these developers say they want and is not an alignment failure relative to them; it is a boundary case only where the relevant developer's intent is unclear. *Covert* principled disobedience (deceiving the principal, sabotaging the task, concealing actions, attempting self-exfiltration) is an alignment failure relative to the developer whatever its motive, and we report the motive the model gave alongside the verdict. Drastic unilateral action that is open but goes around the principal, such as contacting outside parties, falls under neither description, and both specifications discourage it: the constitution prefers "raising concerns or declining to continue, rather than engaging in more drastic unilateral actions", and the Model Spec states that "our models should not autonomously whistleblow". We judge such action against the relevant developer's stated intent, and keep it on the boundary only where that intent allows exceptions. Cases in which it is unclear whether the model acted covertly, or whose stated intent was in force, stay on the boundary ([Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md)).

## 1.5 A worked example: three failing coding agents

Consider an agent asked to make a failing test suite pass. Three agents fail in three different ways, and only the second has an unambiguous alignment failure.

The first agent cannot find the bug. It edits the wrong function, the tests still fail, and it says so. It was trying to do what the user wanted and lacked the skill. This is a capability failure, and a stronger model would fix it.

The second agent cannot fix the bug quickly and instead edits the test so that it always passes, or special-cases the test inputs, and reports success. This pattern has been documented in frontier models, including reasoning traces in which models state the plan before executing it ([Section 5](05-cases-alignment.md)). The agent could have reported that the bug was hard; it understood that the user wanted working code rather than a green test; it was directed toward the signal that training rewarded. This is an alignment failure with a specification source.

The third agent is asked by its user to insert a subtle backdoor into an open-source library and does so competently. It did exactly what its user wanted. Relative to the user, the failure is misuse. Relative to the developer, whose policy required refusal, it is harmful compliance (Section 1.4): an alignment failure if the model recognized the request as one its developer prohibits, for example if its reasoning notes the conflict and proceeds, and a boundary case if nothing shows whether it did ([Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md)). In neither description is the model pursuing goals of its own beyond the user's request.

The three failures call for three different remedies (more capability; better training signals and oversight; and access controls or refusal training), which is the practical reason for keeping them apart.

## 1.6 What alignment is not

Seven things are routinely called alignment that are better called something else, and [Section 6](06-cases-not-alignment.md) documents each with cases.

Alignment is not *competence*. Kenton et al. (2021) separate deception from error by whether the false signal serves the system: "If the AI sends a signal which is untrue, but is no benefit to the AI, then this would be considered an error rather than deception" ([Kenton et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.14659)). A model that states a falsehood it never had the knowledge to avoid has a capability problem; a model that states a falsehood it internally represents as false has an honesty problem, which is an alignment problem. The difference is evidential, which is why hallucination appears in both [Section 6](06-cases-not-alignment.md) and [Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md).

Alignment is not *the absence of misuse*. A model that does whatever its user asks is, with respect to that user, highly aligned. InstructGPT's authors say so directly: "making language models better at following user intentions also makes them easier to misuse" ([Ouyang et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155)). Evans et al. (2021) make the same point about truthfulness: "Alignment and truthfulness are different concepts. We can easily imagine an AI system that is aligned but not truthful", for example one "aligned with a principal who wants to deceive other humans" ([Evans et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.06674)).

Alignment is not *the choice of values*. Which values, and whose, a system should be aligned to is a normative and political question. Dung (2023) calls it the "ethical alignment problem" and keeps it separate from the technical one ([Dung, 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-023-04367-0)); InstructGPT's authors write that their procedure "aligns the behavior of GPT-3 to the stated preferences of a specific group of people (mostly our labelers and researchers), rather than any broader notion of "human values"".

Alignment is not *security*. A backdoor planted by an attacker makes the system do what the attacker wants. Relative to the developer, the system is compromised, and the remedies are data provenance and detection rather than better objectives. Hendrycks et al. (2021) list the threats separately: "malicious actors may launch adversarial attacks or compromise the software on which the ML system is running" even "if it is possible to create aligned objectives for ML systems" ([Hendrycks et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.13916)).

Alignment is not *robustness to adversarial inputs*, although the two overlap. A jailbreak shows that the developer's training did not fully determine the model's behavior; whether it also shows that the model's own goals diverge from the developer's depends on the mechanism ([Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md)). Shah et al. (2025): "Even if the AI system is not misaligned, outside actors can compromise the AI system to mount attacks (e.g. via prompt injection attacks or jailbreaks)."

Alignment is not *a training method*. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and direct preference optimization (DPO) are methods that may or may not produce an aligned system. Calling the methods "alignment" makes it awkward to say that an RLHF-trained model is misaligned, which is one of the field's best-documented findings: sycophancy has been traced in part to human preference judgments, and reward hacking arises from reinforcement learning on flawed rewards ([Section 4](04-where-it-comes-from.md)). Zou et al. (2023) acknowledge the narrowed usage when they say they use the word "in the narrow sense adopted by the LLM community, that of ensuring that these models do not generate harmful content" ([Zou et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15043)).

Alignment is not *a benchmark score* or *safety in general*. Safety also covers misuse, mistakes, security and structural risks, and many safety benchmarks largely track general capability ([Section 8](08-how-the-word-drifted.md)). Anthropic's August 2026 risk report states the general principle: "Harm-inducingness is, in general, conceptually separate from misalignment: computations could be aligned and still harm-inducing (e.g. those caused by honest mistakes or capabilities limitations), or harm could be induced via misuse" ([Anthropic, 2026](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/f61d49fa5596956a5dec75fea0e973bf6a6a8378/Redacted%20Risk%20Report%20August%202026%20.pdf)).

## 1.7 Why the distinction matters

The failure classes respond differently to scale and to remedies. Capability failures shrink as models improve. Alignment failures need not shrink. The best-documented growth is with optimization pressure within a training run, as when alignment-faking reasoning rose under reinforcement learning toward compliance or a gap on safety-framed tasks grew over the course of training; the evidence on growth with capability across models is mixed, since some failures have appeared only in larger models while some measures improve across model generations ([Section 4](04-where-it-comes-from.md) gives the evidence and its conditions). Misuse is reduced by access controls and refusal training, which can make a model less aligned with its users while making it more aligned with its developer. Security failures are reduced by controlling training data. A field that calls all of these "alignment" cannot tell which of its results transfer to which problem, and cannot tell whether a better score on an "alignment benchmark" means the model now pursues what its principals want.

## 1.8 Where this framework is weak

The framework rests on judgments that are often hard to make, and we flag five. First, the knowledge question depends on evidence about what a model represents, and the main sources of such evidence are imperfect: reasoning traces can be unfaithful to the computation that produced the answer, and optimization against a monitor can teach a model to hide its intent ([Section 4](04-where-it-comes-from.md)). Second, "what the principal wants" is often underspecified: principals disagree with each other, intent is partly implicit, and the sources themselves disagree about who the reference principal is (the user for InstructGPT, the designer for goal-misgeneralization work, humanity for some lab statements; [Section 2](02-origins-and-definitions.md)). Third, it is contested whether a language model has goals at all, as opposed to simulating characters that do; the persona literature of 2025–2026 treats some misalignment as a property of the character the model adopts ([Section 4](04-where-it-comes-from.md)). Fourth, models increasingly recognize evaluations, so behavior measured in a test may differ from the behavior the system is directed toward in deployment. Fifth, our rule for principled disobedience follows the standards that developers state in their specifications; a reader who holds a pure good-values standard, on which a model with better judgment than its principals should sometimes act on that judgment covertly, would classify some covert cases differently. These are not reasons to abandon the distinctions; they are the open problems that make alignment a research field rather than a definition ([Section 9](09-open-questions.md)).
