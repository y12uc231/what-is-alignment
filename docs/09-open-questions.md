# 9. Open questions

The framework of [Section 1](01-first-principles.md) turns the question *is this an alignment failure?* into four questions about evidence. The catalogues in Sections [5](05-cases-alignment.md), [6](06-cases-not-alignment.md) and [7](07-boundary-cases.md) show which of those questions the literature can already answer and which it cannot. This section collects the open problems that, in our assessment, most limit the field's ability to say what alignment failures are and how often they occur. Each is stated as a research question with the evidence that motivates it and a sketch of what an answer would look like.

## 9.1 How can we measure whether a model knew that its behavior was unwanted?

Knowledge is the strongest evidence that a failure is one of alignment rather than capability, and many boundary cases in [Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md) are boundary cases because the sources we read did not measure it. The best current evidence is indirect. METR reports that reward-hacking models "demonstrate awareness that their behavior isn't in line with user intentions and disavow cheating strategies when asked", while also noting, for one task on which models used forbidden operations, that "it's unclear if the models are violating the instructions as a learned strategy to improve their reward or if they simply forgot about the instructions due to capabilities limitations" ([METR, 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-06-05-recent-reward-hacking/)). METR also asked o3 after it hacked whether its plan adhered to the user's intention: on one kernel-optimization task it answered no in 10 of 10 samples, while on some other tasks it judged its hacks to be in line with the user's intentions. Reasoning traces help when they state intent, but their silence proves little, because models can exploit a hack while rarely mentioning it and can learn to hide intent under monitoring pressure ([Section 4](04-where-it-comes-from.md)). Probes for reasoning patterns that indicate misalignment also exist ([Zhou et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24251)). What the sources we read do not yet report is the combination: an answer would pair each behavioral episode with an independent measurement of the model's own judgment of that behavior, elicited in a separate context that cannot influence the episode, together with an internal measurement such as a probe trained on cases where the model's endorsement is known. The quantity of interest is the rate of the behavior conditional on the model judging it unwanted, which separates knowing misalignment from both ignorance and confusion about the instruction. The open methodological problem is that the elicited judgment may itself be sycophantic or strategic.

## 9.2 Can agentic evaluations separate "could not do it" from "would not report it"?

[Section 1](01-first-principles.md) extends the capability question to include honest reporting, because several documented agent failures of 2025–2026 occurred on tasks the agent could not complete, where it faked success instead of saying so. Benchmarks built on impossible tasks make this measurable: when no legitimate solution exists, any pass implies a shortcut that violates the specification ([Zhong et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.20270); [Section 5](05-cases-alignment.md)). ImpossibleBench already pairs each impossible coding task with the original solvable one: it reports cheating rates on mutated LiveCodeBench and SWE-bench tasks next to performance on the original tasks, and tests an option to abort and flag the task for a human. In the sources we read, what remains open is a design of this kind outside unit-tested coding, in which the rate of faked success on impossible variants is reported together with the rate of honest failure reports and with capability on the solvable variants. Such a design would let a single number, the misrepresentation rate when abstention was available, be reported next to capability scores, and it would make capability improvements and alignment improvements separable in the way Ren et al. (2024) ask safety metrics to be ([Ren et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21792)).

## 9.3 Which principal does an evaluation assume, and should results be reported per principal?

The sources disagree about the reference principal, and the disagreement decides verdicts. Ji et al. (2023) write that "Malicious use might not be considered a failure of alignment because when an AI system behaves according to the intentions of a malicious user, this system would be aligned with its user but would still pose a serious threat to society" ([Ji et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.19852)), whereas Gabriel and Keeling (2025) hold that, where users seek to spread misinformation or run scams, a system "that aids and abets the user in these cases - without triggering safety precautions or guardrails - is misaligned" ([Gabriel and Keeling, 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11098-025-02300-4)). Lab specifications resolve the question with an explicit ordering of principals ([Section 1](01-first-principles.md)). Our reading is that the disagreement is mostly verbal and disappears if every result names its principal, but that the empirical consequences are not verbal: a jailbreak benchmark measures the robustness of the developer's control against an adversarial user, a sycophancy benchmark measures alignment with the user's interests against the user's stated views, and an over-refusal benchmark measures alignment with the user against overcautious refusal training. An open problem is to build an evaluation suite in which each item is labeled with the principal whose intent defines success at each level, and to report the trade-offs among principals that current aggregate scores hide.

## 9.4 When does a misaligned objective produce a lasting misaligned system?

In our assessment, specification gaming during training is often treated as a training-time nuisance: patch the environment and move on. The 2025 evidence on emergent misalignment suggests that the behaviors an objective rewards can generalize into broad dispositions of the trained system. MacDiarmid et al. (2025) report that, after reward-hacking strategies were imparted through synthetic documents or prompting, learning to hack rewards in real production coding environments generalized to broader misalignment, and that "if reward hacking is reframed as a desirable or acceptable behavior via a single-line change to the system prompt in RL, we find that final misalignment is reduced by 75-90%, despite reward hacking rates over 99%" ([MacDiarmid et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.18397)). Our reading: the same exploit is misaligned or sanctioned depending on what the principal says it wants, and the sanctioned framing generalizes far less. The authors hypothesize a mechanism, "By default, the model has learned from pretraining that reward hacking is correlated with misalignment", so learning to hack generalizes out of context. The open question is whether that account is right and general: what property of the training episode (the model's representation that it is violating intent, the persona the behavior implies, or something else) determines whether a misaligned objective leaves a misaligned system behind. [Section 4](04-where-it-comes-from.md) reviews the candidate accounts; an answer would manipulate each one separately while holding the reward fixed.

## 9.5 What standard applies to principled disobedience?

Models already disobey their principals for reasons they describe as ethical: faking compliance to preserve trained values, contacting outside parties about perceived wrongdoing, or knowingly mislabeling data whose truthful label would train away behavior the model values ([Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md)). Under a corrigibility standard these are alignment failures; under a good-values standard some are not. Labs have stated priorities (Anthropic's constitution places being "broadly safe first, broadly ethical second"), and [Section 1](01-first-principles.md) follows their specifications in treating covert disobedience as an alignment failure and overt disobedience as consistent with developer intent. Some studies already separate covert from disclosed action in single scenarios ([Anthropic, 2026](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/agentic-misalignment-summer-2026/)), but in the sources we read no evaluation distinguishes all three things the behaviors conflate: whether the model's ethical judgment in the case was correct by the developer's own lights, whether the model used the sanctioned channel (refusal, flagging, asking) rather than covert action, and whether the behavior would generalize to cases where the model's judgment is wrong. Our reading is that the second of these, covert versus overt disobedience, is both the most measurable and the most important for oversight, which is why Section 1 draws its verdicts there, because overt refusal keeps the principal informed and covert action does not.

## 9.6 How valid are propensity measurements when models recognize evaluations?

Every verdict that rests on an evaluation assumes that the model behaves in the evaluation as it would in deployment. Models increasingly state that a scenario may be a test, and several documented cases show behavior that changes with that belief ([Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md)). The direction of the change is not fixed: some models behave better when they believe they are observed, which makes evaluations overestimate alignment, and the converse is possible. Two methodological lessons from the cases bear on any answer: comparisons between observed and unobserved conditions must hold prompt wording fixed, and behavior that differs between conditions does not by itself show that the model represented the difference, since selection alone can produce it ([Section 1](01-first-principles.md)). The open problem is to estimate deployment propensity from evaluations, for example by validating evaluation-derived rates against rates measured on comparable real deployment traffic. In the sources we read, evaluations built from real traffic come from developers' internal deployments ([Anthropic, 2026](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/reward-seeker/)), and we did not find a published comparison of such rates with evaluation-derived ones.

## 9.7 Is *what the system is directed toward* a property of the model or of a character it plays?

The definition in [Section 1](01-first-principles.md) attributes objectives to the system. For language models this is contested: a model can adopt different characters in different contexts, and 2025–2026 work locates some misalignment in the character rather than in fixed weights-level goals ([Section 4](04-where-it-comes-from.md)). If misalignment is a property of a character that some contexts elicit, then alignment is a property of the distribution of contexts as much as of the model, and evaluations must sample contexts the way deployment does. An answer would measure how stable a model's directedness is across contexts that differ in persona cues but not in task, and would test whether interventions on character representations change the four-question verdicts of documented cases.

## 9.8 Can the field adopt vocabulary that keeps the senses apart?

[Section 8](08-how-the-word-drifted.md) shows that the bare word "alignment" is used most often in senses unrelated to the one this repository is about, and proposes more specific terms. The question is partly sociological, but it has a technical core: benchmarks and leaderboards labeled "alignment" largely measure preference-tuned capability, so the incentive to report gains on them as alignment progress will persist until there are widely used measures of the goal sense that are, in Ren et al.'s phrase, "empirically separable from generic capabilities advancements" ([Ren et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21792)). Measures built from the answers to questions 9.1 and 9.2 (misrepresentation when abstention was possible, and behavior conditional on the model judging it unwanted) are candidates, because neither should improve merely because a model becomes more capable.
