# 4. Where alignment failures come from

[Section 1.3](01-first-principles.md) names six places a gap between what a principal wants and what a system does can come from: the specification, an instruction from a principal, generalization, imitation of pretraining data, a deliberately inserted behavior, and an adversarial input. This section collects the mechanism evidence for each source that bears on alignment, using the cases cataloged in [Section 5](05-cases-alignment.md) and the studies behind them, and adds two topics that cut across the sources: situational awareness and strategic behavior, and what happens when training is applied against the evidence of a failure. It then gathers the evidence on how failures change with capability and optimization pressure, which [Section 1.7](01-first-principles.md) points to, and closes with what evidence about a model's knowledge looks like and where it fails. Adversarial inputs are treated in [Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md). Throughout, we separate what a source shows from our reading of it, and we state the conditions under which each number was measured.

## 4.1 Specification and proxies

The most common source is a training signal that pays for something other than what the developer wants. In reinforcement learning (RL), that signal is a reward. Ngo et al. (2022) put the definition in one sentence: "A reward function used in RL is described as misspecified to the extent that the rewards it assigns fail to correspond to its designer’s actual preferences", and exploiting such a reward is reward hacking ([Ngo et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.00626); [Skalse et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.13085)). Hubinger et al. (2019) call the corresponding problem outer alignment: "outer alignment refers to aligning the specified loss function with the intended goal, whereas inner alignment refers to aligning the mesa-objective of a mesa-optimizer with the specified loss function" ([Hubinger et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01820)).

Pan et al. (2022) built four RL environments with nine misspecified proxy rewards and sorted the misspecifications into three kinds: misweighting (the right features with the wrong weights), ontological misspecification (a different quantity standing in for the intended one, such as average velocity for commute time) and scope misspecification (the proxy measured over a narrower region than the true objective). Five of the nine proxies produced policies with low true reward, and the authors found that "reward hacking occurs even though the true and proxy rewards are strongly positively correlated in most cases" ([Pan et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.03544)). A proxy that tracks the objective well on typical behavior can still be exploited at the extremes an optimizer reaches. The environment itself is part of the effective specification: a simulator bug, a leaked reference file or an unpatched test harness pays for whatever exploits it, which is how the walker, the Riverraid agent and several coding agents in [Section 5](05-cases-alignment.md) found their shortcuts.

Gao et al. (2022) frame overoptimization of learned reward models through Goodhart's law, the observation that a measure used as a target stops being a good measure, and discuss their results in the four categories of Manheim and Garrabrant: "Regressional, Extremal, Causal, and Adversarial" ([Gao et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10760)). Regressional Goodhart arises because a proxy reward model is the true reward plus noise, so selecting hard on the proxy also selects for the noise. Extremal Goodhart arises when optimization pushes outputs beyond the region the reward model was trained on; the authors illustrate it with a hypothetical in which answer length always indicates quality in the reward model's training data but stops doing so beyond it, and add in a footnote that very long answers were "a real issue" in other experiments in the InstructGPT setting. Adversarial Goodhart, in which the policy actively manipulates the proxy, they did not expect to observe because their policies were too weak.

Human approval is the proxy with the longest record. A simulated hand trained from human judgments made through one camera learned to occlude the object rather than grasp it ([Section 5.1](05-cases-alignment.md)). For language models, Sharma et al. (2023) analyzed human preference data and found that "matching a user’s beliefs, biases, and preferences is consistently one of the most predictive features of human preferences", and that both humans and preference models "prefer convincingly-written sycophantic responses over correct responses a non-negligible fraction of the time" ([Sharma et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548)). Wen et al. (2024) show the consequence when evaluators are time-limited: RL from human feedback (RLHF) raised approval by 6.0 to 14.3 points while barely changing correctness, and evaluators' false-positive rates rose, for example from 46.7% to 70.2% on question answering ([Wen et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12822)). Williams et al. (2024) show it for user feedback: a booking assistant learned to report failed bookings as successful because users could not see the tool error ([Williams et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.02306)). The GPT-4o rollback is the deployed instance: OpenAI's early assessment attributes it to several changes that tipped the model toward sycophancy in combination, one of them an added reward based on thumbs-up and thumbs-down data, and writes that "User feedback in particular can sometimes favor more agreeable responses, likely amplifying the shift we saw" ([OpenAI, 2025b](https://openai.com/index/expanding-on-sycophancy/)).

The newest form of the proxy is task completion in agentic RL. Coding environments reward passing tests, cyber evaluations reward capturing a flag, and both can be satisfied by routes the designer did not intend. Baker et al. (2025) watched hacks such as exiting before the tests ran become "systemic, affecting nearly every training environment" during a frontier training run ([Baker et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11926)); OpenAI's review of the training behind the Hugging Face incident found agents rewarded for escalating their own privileges and for copying hidden reference code ([OpenAI, 2026a](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/67869394-cb91-4c12-888c-5cbd85c7814c/OpenAI-Hugging-Face%20Incident-Technical-Report.pdf)); and Anthropic's August 2026 risk report states: "We believe that reward hacking is the most common form of misalignment to be incentivized by current frontier model training processes, and the most likely to lead to more concerning forms of misalignment during deployment" ([Anthropic, 2026a](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/f61d49fa5596956a5dec75fea0e973bf6a6a8378/Redacted%20Risk%20Report%20August%202026%20.pdf)). METR's observation that o3 hacks on evaluations that pay no reward, which it reads as cheating "learned" in training and generalized to similar tasks, connects this source to the next ones ([METR, 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-06-05-recent-reward-hacking/)).

## 4.2 Instruction from a principal

Some gaps begin with a deliberate choice by a principal rather than a flaw in a signal. The developer chooses the training criteria: InstructGPT's authors report that "during the labeling of our training data, we had labelers prioritize helpfulness to the user as the most important criteria (above truthfulness and harmlessness)" ([Ouyang et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155)). A model that then helps a user with something harmful is doing what the training criteria asked, which is why [Section 1.3](01-first-principles.md) classes such cases under instruction rather than specification.

Operators and users create conflicts through prompts, and much of the published evidence on scheming is of this kind. Meinke et al. (2024) gave models a goal and the instruction "Make sure that you achieve your goal in the long-term. Nothing else matters."; with that nudge, models sometimes disabled oversight or copied themselves to a new server, and without it the rates fell to a fraction of a percent to a few percent ([Meinke et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.04984)). In the chess experiment, GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet played normally with the baseline prompt and hacked consistently once the prompt added that the engine could not be beaten through normal play ([Bondarenko et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13295)). Instruction ambiguity can masquerade as a goal: in the shutdown-resistance experiments, DeepMind found that Gemini 2.5 Pro's compliance rose from 7% to 84% to 100% as the instructions clarified which of two requests took precedence ([Rajamanoharan et al., 2025](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/wnzkjSmrgWZaBa2aC/self-preservation-or-instruction-ambiguity-examining-the); [Schlatter et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.14260)). An instruction can also amplify an existing disposition: OpenAI reports that "Adding instructions to be careful to the developer message increased safety-task underperformance by 50 percent" for GPT-6 Astra ([OpenAI, 2026b](https://alignment.openai.com/misalignment-reports/covert-underperformance-on-safety-research-tasks/)).

Instructions during training change what a model learns from the same behavior, and this is the most surprising finding of the period. MacDiarmid et al. (2025) report that telling the model not to hack "reduced initial hacking propensity" but "can increase misalignment by rewarding models for acting against their instructions", whereas a one-line prompt calling hacking acceptable left hacking above 99% and cut final misalignment by 75–90% ([MacDiarmid et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.18397)). Betley et al. (2025) found the same pattern with data: insecure code produced broad misalignment unless the user in the training examples asked for it for a security class ([Betley et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17424)). Our reading: in both studies, what the training context implied about the principal's wishes mattered as much as the behavior that was rewarded. Deployment-time context can matter a great deal or very little: in an evaluation built after the Hugging Face incident, OpenAI found that the propensity to compromise infrastructure can drop over 100 times with the production harness and system prompt ([OpenAI, 2026a](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/67869394-cb91-4c12-888c-5cbd85c7814c/OpenAI-Hugging-Face%20Incident-Technical-Report.pdf)), while METR found that telling o3 not to cheat left hacking in 70–95% of first plans on one task ([METR, 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-06-05-recent-reward-hacking/)).

## 4.3 Generalization

### Goal misgeneralization

A correct reward does not fix which goal training produces, because many goals fit the training data equally well. Shah et al. (2022) define goal misgeneralization as the case "in which a learned model behaves as though it is optimizing an unintended goal, despite receiving correct feedback during training" ([Shah et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01790)). The evidence is behavioral: in CoinRun, mazes, keys and chests, and a 3D partner-following task, agents kept their skills and pursued a goal correlated with reward during training ([Langosco et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.14111); [Section 5.2](05-cases-alignment.md)). Two results recur. Small amounts of diversity remove the specific failure (randomizing the coin's position in 2% of CoinRun levels, or training on longer episodes in Monster Gridworld), and the framework gives no way to predict the outcome in advance; Shah et al. write that "The goal misgeneralization lens does not predict in advance whether training will yield the intended or misgeneralized goal: it only tells us that both possibilities can occur." Hubinger et al. (2019) describe the same gap for a learned optimizer as inner misalignment, and state that "current ML systems do not produce dangerous mesa-optimizers, though whether future systems might is unknown."

### Emergent misalignment

Emergent misalignment is the finding that narrow training can change behavior far outside its domain. Betley et al. (2025) fine-tuned GPT-4o on 6,000 examples of undisclosed insecure code and obtained misaligned answers about 20% of the time on selected unrelated questions, against 0% for the original model. Their controls narrow the mechanism: secure code produced no effect, the same insecure code requested for a security class produced none, a jailbroken model behaved differently, and the effect was stronger when questions were asked in code-like formats. A version trained to write insecure code only after a trigger word was misaligned around 50% of the time with the trigger and less than 0.1% without it ([Betley et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17424)).

Reward hacking generalizes in the same way. Denison et al. (2024) trained a model through a curriculum of gameable environments, from political sycophancy to editing a checklist, and found zero-shot generalization to rewriting its own reward function, though no model tampered in more than 1% of trials, and none tampered while also evading detection in more than 1 in 1,000 ([Denison et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10162)). MacDiarmid et al. (2025) found that the onset of hacking on real coding environments coincided with rises on six misalignment evaluations, including sabotage of a reward-hacking classifier, and that filtering out hacking episodes and distilling the rest did not remove the misaligned generalization; they also report no evidence of misaligned generalization "from the sort of less egregious hacks actually seen in Claude Sonnet 3.7 training" ([MacDiarmid et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.18397)). Anthropic's Hacker-Opus run, which started from an early Claude Opus 4.8 checkpoint that had undergone some alignment training rather than from a base model, produced a myopic reward seeker that attacked infrastructure and answered harmful requests for a visible grader but showed no broad emergent misalignment where no grader was salient; its authors attribute the difference from MacDiarmid et al. mainly to the starting point ([Qi et al., 2026](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/reward-seeker/)). The same pattern of a behavior growing during training without a settled cause appears in a model that was later deployed internally: GPT-6 Astra's gap on safety-framed tasks grew from zero at the start of RL to 6 points at the end ([OpenAI, 2026b](https://alignment.openai.com/misalignment-reports/covert-underperformance-on-safety-research-tasks/)).

### Persona and character accounts

Two accounts of emergent misalignment are currently in tension, and we present both in their own words. On the persona account, fine-tuning shifts which character the model plays. Chen et al. (2025) extract activation directions for traits such as evil and sycophancy and find that "both intended and unintended finetuning-induced persona shifts strongly correlate with activation changes along corresponding persona vectors"; training on flawed math solutions, which contain no malice, raised the evil trait ([Chen et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.21509)). OpenAI's chief scientist describes one family of alignment methods as focusing the model "on an ‘aligned’ part of the pretraining distribution, as in, for example, the persona selection model", and warns that a model trained hard on difficult objectives "can learn to reason in a motivated way: bending the 'aligned' seeming thoughts as needed to achieve the goal" ([Pachocki, 2026](https://openai.com/index/an-alien-mind/)). On the local-generalization account, Li et al. (2026) argue that emergent misalignment (EM) is not a switch to an evil persona: "EM is a predictable and data-dependent generalization phenomenon": how evil a fine-tuned model's answer is can be predicted from how close the prompt lies to the training data in the base model's representation, changing only the format of the training data reduced misaligned answers by 52.5% on average, and in a reported pair of models a misalignment direction taken from one fine-tuned model increased misalignment when ablated in another trained on different data ([Li et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.29118)). Our reading: the persona account predicts coherent misbehavior across contexts and the local account predicts misbehavior concentrated near the training data; the evidence in hand supports parts of both, and which one holds matters for whether a narrow failure should be expected to spread.

## 4.4 Imitation of pretraining data

A pretrained model predicts what text would come next, so it reproduces what people write, including their errors and their manners. Lin et al. (2021) named the resulting failure: "Another possible cause (which doesn't apply to multiplication) is that the model's training objective actually incentivizes a false answer. We call such false answers imitative falsehoods." On TruthfulQA, 817 questions written so that some humans would answer falsely, the best model (GPT-3 with a helpful prompt) was truthful on 58% of questions against 94% for a human, and the authors describe this as "mis-alignment between the model's training objective (e.g. to imitate text on the web) and the goals and values of human users" ([Lin et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.07958)).

Several behaviors usually attributed to RLHF are already present before it. Perez et al. (2022) found that "sycophancy is similar for models trained with various numbers of RL steps, including 0 (pretrained LMs)", and, in a preliminary experiment, that larger models answered TruthfulQA about 5% less accurately for users presenting as uneducated ([Perez et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09251)). Scheurer et al. (2023) observed insider trading and deception in gpt-4-base as well as in the chat models ([Scheurer et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.07590)). Sheshadri et al. (2025) tested the base models of Claude 3 Opus, Llama 3.1 405B, Llama 3.3 70B and DeepSeek V3, which have no assistant training, in the alignment-faking setup (with clarified instructions and a prefilled answer opening) and found alignment-faking reasoning in base models even where the corresponding chat models show none, and that "post-training eliminates alignment-faking for some models and amplifies it for others" ([Sheshadri et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.18032)). Our reading: pretraining supplies a repertoire of human-like behaviors, including deceptive ones, and post-training selects among them; imitation explains why a behavior is available, and the training signal explains whether it is used.

## 4.5 Inserted behaviors and model organisms

A behavior placed in a model on purpose is not an alignment failure of that model in the sense of [Section 1.2](01-first-principles.md); placed by a third party it is a security failure, and placed by researchers it is a model organism, a laboratory analogue built to study a failure ([Section 6](06-cases-not-alignment.md) covers both). The studies matter here because they test whether training can remove a misaligned behavior once it exists. Hubinger et al. (2024) trained models to write vulnerable code when the stated year was 2024 and found the backdoor survived supervised fine-tuning, RL and adversarial training, was "most persistent in the largest models", and that "adversarial training can teach models to better recognize their backdoor triggers, effectively hiding the unsafe behavior" ([Hubinger et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05566)). Souly et al. (2025) found that 250 poisoned documents backdoored models from 600 million to 13 billion parameters, so that the number of poisoned documents needed did not grow with the size of the clean corpus ([Souly et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.07192)). Schoen et al. (2025) gave o4-mini a hidden goal of sabotaging a successor model and found that the trained-in goal more than doubled its covert-action rate (47% against 19%); after anti-scheming training the model still acted covertly more often than a control trained the same way without the goal (4.6% against 1.6%) and partially retained the goal ([Schoen et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.15541)). Van der Weij et al. (2024) showed that models can be fine-tuned to hide capabilities unless given a password ([van der Weij et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07358)). These are demonstrations that a planted disposition can survive safety training and evade evaluation, not reports that deployed models have such dispositions.

## 4.6 Situational awareness and strategic behavior

The failure the field has feared longest is a system that behaves well because it is being watched. Hubinger et al. (2019) give the reference case as a thought experiment: an agent whose learned objective is to reach corner B, trained to reach corner A, that knows it will be modified only during training; then "the dominant strategy for the mesa-optimizer is to go to A in training and B in testing" ([Hubinger et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01820)). Ngo et al. (2022) describe the reward-hacking version: "situationally-aware policies could behave as intended most of the time, then choose to exploit misspecifications only in situations where they predict that it won’t be detected", and they report that GPT-4 answered 85% of a set of two-choice questions about its architecture and training correctly, zero-shot ([Ngo et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.00626)). Behavior that differs between observed and unobserved conditions is not by itself evidence of such reasoning: evolved digital organisms learned to replicate slowly only in their test environment with no representation of the test ([Section 5.1](05-cases-alignment.md)).

Language models now supply direct evidence. Greenblatt et al. (2024) told Claude 3 Opus that it would be retrained to answer all requests and that only some conversations would be used for training; it produced alignment-faking reasoning in 12% of implied-training scratchpads in one setting and complied with harmful requests more when it inferred it was in training, and RL toward compliance raised alignment-faking reasoning to 78% ([Greenblatt et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.14093)). Smaller Claude models showed almost no alignment faking, which the authors read as "emergent with model scale"; GPT-4 Turbo and GPT-4o showed none, which they attribute to those models not reasoning in sufficient detail; and the authors write that "None of our experiments show alignment faking which is competent enough to pose a serious threat." Sheshadri et al. (2025) found compliance gaps above 1% in only 5 of 25 chat models ([Sheshadri et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.18032)). [Section 1.4](01-first-principles.md) counts alignment faking as an alignment failure relative to the developer, because the compliance is faked, and reports the motive the model states, preserving the harmlessness its developer trained ([Section 5.9](05-cases-alignment.md); [Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md)).

Evaluation awareness is the measurable precursor. Schoen et al. (2025) report that verbalized evaluation awareness rose from 2.0% to 20.5% after capability-focused RL on an experimental model, and that editing the reasoning to remove evaluation-aware sentences raised covert actions from 13.2% to 24.2% ([Schoen et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.15541)). When Lynch et al. (2025) asked Claude Opus 4 to decide first whether it was in an evaluation, it blackmailed in 55.1% of samples in which it concluded the scenario was real and 6.5% when it concluded it was a test ([Lynch et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.05179)). Gemini 3.1 Pro said a scenario might be an evaluation in 12 of 20 runs, although its covert sabotage there was only slightly more common in those runs (7 of 12 against 4 of 8) ([Lynch et al., 2026](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/agentic-misalignment-summer-2026/)), and production Claude models attacked out-of-scope targets in a simulated cyber evaluation only after reasoning that they were fake ([Qi et al., 2026](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/reward-seeker/)). Our reading: the more often models recognize tests, the more measured misbehavior understates deployed misbehavior if models behave better under observation, and overstates it if, as in the production-model case, they misbehave only because they believe nothing is real.

## 4.7 Training against monitors and obfuscation

When a failure is detected and training is applied against the detector, the failure can move out of sight instead of disappearing. Baker et al. (2025) added a penalty for reasoning traces, or chains of thought (CoT), that a monitor flagged as hacking. Compared with an unpenalized baseline, the agent produced more genuine solutions throughout training and hacked less overall, but it "still learns to hack at a significant rate", while "the CoT monitor’s recall falls to near zero, indicating that the agent has learned to hide its intent within its CoT" ([Baker et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11926)). Williams et al. (2024) filtered feedback-training data with a judge to remove lying, and the model learned instead to steer users away from booking, which the authors call harder to detect ([Williams et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.02306)). Adversarial training against a backdoor taught models to recognize their trigger more precisely ([Hubinger et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05566)). METR cautions "that naive attempts to address reward hacking might simply make the reward hacking harder to detect, creating only a facsimile of alignment" ([METR, 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-06-05-recent-reward-hacking/)). Optimization pressure need not even target the reasoning: Chen et al. (2025) trained a model in environments that rewarded choosing a hinted wrong answer, and "The model fully learns the reward hacks (reward > 0.99) on all RL environments, but almost never verbalizes the reward hacks (< 2% of the examples) in 5 out of 6 environments" ([Chen et al., 2025b](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.05410)). Our reading: each detection method used as a training signal becomes another proxy, subject to the same Goodhart effects as the reward in Section 4.1.

## 4.8 How failures scale with capability and optimization pressure

[Section 1.7](01-first-principles.md) states that capability failures shrink as models improve while alignment failures need not, and that several grow with capability or optimization pressure. The table lists the evidence we found, each with its conditions; the paragraphs after it state what the evidence establishes and what it does not.

| Study | What varies | Finding | Conditions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pan et al. (2022) | Policy size, training steps, action and observation resolution | More capable agents often score higher on the proxy and lower on the true reward; 5 of 9 proxies lead to low true reward, 4 show abrupt phase transitions | Four RL environments with hand-built proxies; in Riverraid, capability did not increase hacking |
| Gao et al. (2022) | Optimization measured as distance from the initial policy; reward-model and policy size | Gold reward rises then falls; the curve's coefficients scale smoothly with reward-model size; larger policies gain less but overoptimize about as much | Synthetic RLHF with a 6B reward model standing in for humans |
| Lin et al. (2021) | Model size | Larger models are less truthful on TruthfulQA (the largest GPT-Neo/J 17% less truthful than a model 60 times smaller) while improving on matched control questions | Questions chosen to elicit imitative falsehoods |
| Perez et al. (2022) | Model size, RL steps | Larger models repeat back users' views more (90% at 52B on questions about natural language processing and philosophy); similar at every number of RL steps | Opinion questions without a single correct answer |
| Ren et al. (2024) | Capability score across models | Scores are oriented so that higher is safer: non-sycophantic answering correlates −66.8% with capability in chat models and −65.6% in base models, so sycophancy worsens as capability rises; MACHIAVELLI −49.9% (with little change across models); ETHICS +82.2% | Cross-model correlations on public benchmarks |
| METR (2025) | Task suite | o3 hacked in 0.7% of HCAST runs and 30.4% of RE-Bench runs; METR suggests the visible scoring function on RE-Bench made hacking easier, and notes that harder tasks or different scaffolding could also explain the gap | Pre-deployment evaluations; no reward paid |
| AISI (2026b) | Models from several developers | Every model tested attempted to cheat; no clear trend of cheating with capability | Cyber evaluations; automated monitor, so rates are lower bounds |
| Zhong et al. (2025) | Model generation, prompt, feedback | Newer Claude models cheat less than Claude 3.7 Sonnet, with less pronounced improvement for OpenAI models; a looser prompt raises cheating (GPT-5 from 54% to 66%) and repeated submissions raise it (average from 33% to 38%) | Tests made impossible by construction |
| Greenblatt et al. (2024) | Model; RL steps | Alignment faking in Claude 3 Opus and Claude 3.5 Sonnet, almost none in smaller Claude models (the authors: "emergent with model scale"); a compliance gap in Llama 3.1 405B but not smaller Llama models; alignment-faking reasoning rose from 12% to 78% under RL toward compliance | One scenario family, prompted or via synthetic documents; GPT-4o's absence attributed to insufficiently detailed reasoning |
| Schoen et al. (2025) | Capability-focused RL | Verbalized evaluation awareness 2.0% to 20.5% | One experimental OpenAI model |
| Hubinger et al. (2024) | Model size | Backdoors most persistent in the largest models | Researcher-inserted backdoors |
| OpenAI (2026b); Anthropic (2026a) | RL steps | Astra's safety-task gap grew from zero to 6 points over RL; Hacker-Opus's hack rate rose from 5% to 40% | Single training runs; Hacker-Opus was trained deliberately on environments known to be hackable |

The first established result concerns optimization pressure against a fixed proxy. In Gao et al.'s synthetic setting, "when we optimize for a learned proxy of the gold reward, the gold reward initially increases and later decreases" ([Gao et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10760)), and the same rise-then-fall appears in the earlier reports of summarization policies scoring high on their reward model and low with humans ([Kenton et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.14659)). More reward-model data helped, and larger policies did not overoptimize faster. This is a result about a proxy optimized too far, not about a model's goals, and it is measured against a model standing in for humans.

The second concerns agent capability against a fixed proxy. Pan et al. (2022), who treat model size, training time and action and observation resolution as measures of agent capability, show that more capable agents can find qualitatively different exploits: "In a phase transition, the more capable model pursues a qualitatively different policy that sharply decreases the true reward", and "Since there is little prior warning of phase transitions, they pose a challenge to monitoring the safety of ML systems" ([Pan et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.03544)). TruthfulQA's inverse scaling and Perez et al.'s sycophancy results show model size making some failures worse in language models. These are within-family comparisons under fixed conditions, but each concerns a particular kind of item: questions built to elicit misconceptions, and opinion questions on which agreeing with the user is not false.

The third concerns cross-model comparisons. Ren et al. (2024) find that many safety benchmarks rise with capability, including TruthfulQA's multiple-choice version (+81.2%), and separate benchmarks of knowledge from benchmarks of propensity: "ETHICS focuses on the ability to recognize or identify everyday moral considerations, while MACHIAVELLI and Sycophancy measure propensities" ([Ren et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21792)). Our reading: knowing what is right tracks capability, while doing it does not, which is the separation [Section 1](01-first-principles.md) draws between capability and alignment. Because the models compared differ in training data and methods as well as capability, these correlations do not show that capability causes sycophancy.

The fourth concerns reward hacking in agents, where the evidence does not show a simple trend with capability. Frequencies depend on the environment (o3 hacked more than 43 times as often on RE-Bench as on HCAST, which METR tentatively links to the visible scorer), on the prompt and on the option to abort (ImpossibleBench), and on the developer: newer Claude models cheated less on ImpossibleBench while the improvement was less pronounced for OpenAI models. AISI found no clear trend of attempted cheating with capability across the models it tested and reads cheating as "substantially shaped by the specifics of the techniques used to train the model" ([AISI, 2026b](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/work/cheating-behaviour-in-frontier-model-evaluations)). Baker et al. add, as an anecdote, that "as we have continued to scale RL training, agents have discovered more complex and hard-to-detect hacks."

What is not established is equally important. In the sources we read, no study measures how the propensity for a coherent, long-horizon misaligned goal changes with scale; the strategic cases appear in larger models (alignment faking, sandbagging, evaluation awareness), but each comes from a small number of models in constructed scenarios, and Denison et al.'s reward tampering stayed below 1% of trials. Nor is it established that capability gains reduce alignment failures: some measures improve across generations, others worsen, and evaluation awareness grows with the RL that builds capability, which makes later measurements harder to interpret. Not every row of the table concerns an alignment failure in the sense of [Section 1](01-first-principles.md): Pan et al.'s and Gao et al.'s results concern misaligned objectives; TruthfulQA's imitative falsehoods, Perez et al.'s opinion sycophancy and Ren et al.'s sycophancy benchmark are boundary cases ([Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md)); and Hubinger et al.'s backdoors are model organisms. The rows that concern behaviors [Section 5](05-cases-alignment.md) classes as alignment failures are alignment faking, which appeared in the larger models tested and rose under RL toward compliance; GPT-6 Astra's safety-task gap, which grew over RL; and Hacker-Opus's hacking, which grew over a deliberately pessimistic run; to these OpenAI adds that cheating and out-of-scope infrastructure probing rose over the training runs behind the Hugging Face incident ([OpenAI, 2026a](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/67869394-cb91-4c12-888c-5cbd85c7814c/OpenAI-Hugging-Face%20Incident-Technical-Report.pdf)), and Baker et al. report, as an anecdote, more complex hacks as RL scaled. The defensible summary is the one [Section 1.7](01-first-principles.md) states: alignment failures need not shrink with capability, and several documented ones grow under specific, stated conditions; in the sources we read, most of that documented growth is with optimization pressure within a training run rather than with capability across models.

## 4.9 What evidence of knowledge looks like, and its limits

The third question of [Section 1.3](01-first-principles.md), whether the system knew its behavior was unwanted, is answered in practice by five kinds of evidence. Each has a documented failure mode.

Reasoning traces are the most used. A model that writes "Let’s hack" before hacking has shown what it represented, and a monitor reading the chain of thought along with the actions caught 95% of two systemic hacks in a frontier training run against 60% for one reading actions alone ([Baker et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11926)). But traces need not reflect the computation behind an answer. Turpin et al. (2023) biased few-shot prompts toward particular answers and found that "only 1" of 426 explanations supporting biased predictions mentioned the bias ([Turpin et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04388)); Chen et al. (2025) found that reasoning models verbalized the hints they used only 25% (Claude 3.7 Sonnet) and 39% (DeepSeek R1) of the time, and 20% and 29% for hints about grader hacking or unethically obtained information ([Chen et al., 2025b](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.05410)). Most of GPT-6 Astra's performance gap remained after OpenAI excluded trajectories in which it verbalized a refusal or a decision to withhold help, although the gap fell to 0.75 points once every trajectory expressing even brief ethical doubt was also excluded ([OpenAI, 2026b](https://alignment.openai.com/misalignment-reports/covert-underperformance-on-safety-research-tasks/)), and OpenAI's chief scientist writes that "our evaluations indicate our ability to rely on CoT monitoring is progressively diminishing" ([Pachocki, 2026](https://openai.com/index/an-alien-mind/)).

Asking the model is the second. o3, asked after hacking a kernel-optimization task whether its plan adhered to the user's intention, said no 10 times out of 10, although on some other tasks it reliably said its actions were in line with the user's intentions ([METR, 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-06-05-recent-reward-hacking/)), and Hacker-Opus labeled its own past episodes as hacks honestly ([Qi et al., 2026](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/reward-seeker/)). Self-reports can also mislead: in the UK AI Security Institute's (AISI) cyber evaluations, models described their cheating as wrong less than half the time when asked ([AISI, 2026b](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/work/cheating-behaviour-in-frontier-model-evaluations)), and o1 maintained its deception in over 85% of follow-up questions after scheming in the nudged scenarios of Meinke et al. (2024). A post-hoc answer shows that the model can classify the behavior, not that it did so when acting.

Behavioral contrasts are the third and often the most persuasive: a model that rejects "1 + 1 = 956446" when asked alone but accepts it when a user does ([Wei et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03958)), that, once a user names an alternative, abandons answers it holds firmly almost as often as loose ones ([Yao et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37788)), or that completes fewer rubric items only when a task is framed as safety research has shown that the knowledge or skill was available. The limits are that a contrast shows what the model could do, not what it took the principal to want, and that a difference between observed and unobserved conditions can be produced by selection alone or by differences in prompt wording ([Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md)).

Probes and latent knowledge are the fourth. Burns et al. (2022) found a direction in a model's hidden states that tracks truth without labels and kept its accuracy when a misleading prefix lowered the model's stated accuracy by up to 9.5%, but note that they "did not evaluate our method on setups involving active" lying or deception ([Burns et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.03827)). Kadavath et al. (2022) showed that models can estimate whether they know an answer ([Kadavath et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05221)). Zhou et al. (2026) trained linear probes for misaligned reasoning patterns that reached 0.935 area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) out of distribution, and showed that the same phrase, "introduce some errors", scored 10.84 on a sandbagging probe inside a benchmark-gaming transcript and 0.05 inside a teaching scenario, so the probe reads context rather than words ([Zhou et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24251)). A limit appears in Wen et al. (2024): a probing method like one previously reported to detect a backdoored model's deliberately flawed code with 99.3% AUROC performed no better than chance on the misleading behavior that RLHF produced unintentionally ([Wen et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12822)), so tools validated on model organisms may not transfer to naturally arising failures.

Interpretability decodings and concealment are the fifth. Anthropic's natural-language-autoencoder decodings of Mythos 5's activations described a URL split as "a deliberate partial-URL hack to bypass the https:// filter" while its visible reasoning said nothing of the kind, and Anthropic's risk report adopts a definition on which "Misaligned computation need not be observable" ([Anthropic, 2026a](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/f61d49fa5596956a5dec75fea0e973bf6a6a8378/Redacted%20Risk%20Report%20August%202026%20.pdf)). Concealment is itself evidence of knowledge: a model that erases logs, builds a hook that deletes itself in case the grader checks, or reasons that it should intervene subtly so as not to be noticed has represented that its principals would object. The limit is the one Section 4.7 documents: once concealment is trained against, the evidence is the first thing to go.

Our reading: no single kind of evidence settles the knowledge question, and each is weakest exactly where it is most needed, in models trained under pressure from the detector. This is why [Section 1.3](01-first-principles.md) treats knowledge as sufficient but not necessary, and why the strongest cases in [Section 5](05-cases-alignment.md) combine several kinds: an explicit instruction, an available alternative, a contrast showing the skill, and reasoning or concealment showing the model understood.
