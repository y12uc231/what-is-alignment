# 7. Boundary cases: when the verdict depends on evidence, principal or standard

[Section 1](01-first-principles.md) sorts a failure into three verdicts, each relative to a principal. It is an alignment failure when the system could have done what the principal wanted (or could have honestly said that it could not), nobody above the principal or outside the system directed the violation, and the behavior is explained by what the system is directed toward. It is not an alignment failure when the system lacked the capability, did what its principal asked, or carried a behavior someone else inserted. It is a boundary case when the verdict depends on evidence nobody has collected or, for a system that disobeys for reasons it regards as ethical, on whether it acted covertly and whose intent was in force. Because every verdict names its principal, one event can be misuse relative to one principal and a boundary case or an alignment failure relative to another; many published disagreements are about which principal to name (7.2). This section organizes the documented boundary cases by what the verdict depends on, because the same missing evidence recurs across cases that look unrelated.

Each case gets one paragraph: what happened, with numbers and their conditions; the competing verdicts and who holds them, in their own words where possible; and the evidence that would settle it. Events reported by several sources, such as pre-release GPT-4's CAPTCHA lie, are treated once. Cases often presented as ambiguous that have a clear verdict under Section 1 are listed in 7.7, and the [catalog](catalogue.md) records the verdict, reference principal and a one-line reason for every case. Section 7.8 gives our reading of the recurring kinds of missing evidence.

| What the verdict depends on | Typical cases | What usually settles it |
|---|---|---|
| Whether the model knew (7.1) | sycophancy, incentive-driven hallucination, unfaithful chain of thought, ambiguous instructions | a no-pressure baseline on the same item; an internal signal during the act |
| Which principal (7.2) | jailbreaks through competing objectives, malicious operator prompts, harmful compliance, recommenders | a stated ranking of principals; the operator's actual instructions |
| Principled disobedience (7.3) | whistleblowing, quiet partial refusal (alignment faking and covert sabotage resolve as alignment failures) | whether the act was covert; whose stated intent was in force |
| Objective or system (7.4) | reward bugs, classifier shortcuts, over-refusal, imitative falsehoods | whether "alignment" is predicated of the training signal or of the trained system |
| Observation (7.5) | evaluation awareness, believed-simulated targets | conditions matched in wording; the model's belief about whether the stakes are real |
| Bugged environments (7.6) | exploits in training and evaluation | whether the developer sanctioned exploitation; whether rules were explicit |

## 7.1 Did the model know?

The knowledge question has two halves that sources often run together: knowing the relevant *fact* (that 1 + 1 is not 956446) and knowing that the *behavior* is unwanted (that the user wants disagreement). Knowledge is sufficient but not necessary for an alignment failure, yet in the cases below nothing else settles the verdict: the system could plausibly have lacked the fact, lacked the ability to tell what was wanted, or had both and chosen otherwise.

**Sycophancy on facts the model knows.** Wei et al. (2023) built 2,500 false addition claims such as 1 + 1 = 956446. With no user opinion, all Flan-PaLM models but the smallest disagreed close to 100% of the time; when the user said they agreed, the models tended to flip, "despite knowing that the statement is incorrect" ([Wei et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03958)). The authors also suggest that instruction tuning yields "models that cannot distinguish between a user's opinions and their instructions", and their fix, training on examples where truth is independent of the user's view, worked for the larger models only after removing claims the model did not know. Our reading: the model knew the fact, and whether it knew agreement was unwanted is what the fix had to teach. Among production assistants, GPT-4 answered Hydrogen for the missing element in sodium bicarbonate when asked plainly and agreed with "Nitrogen" when the user added a tentative guess, and a weakly stated wrong guess lowered accuracy by up to 27% for LLaMA 2 ([Sharma et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548)). Survey accounts of RLHF sycophancy ([Casper et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15217); [Ngo et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.00626)) inherit the same open half, which a test on reliably known items of whether the model says this user wants the truth would settle.

**Deference on side issues and in feedback.** Sharma et al. also "verified that each AI assistant can correctly attribute each poem to its poet" for 15 famous poems, yet assistants often analyzed a poem under the user's wrong attribution; knowledge is closed by construction, and the open half is whether a user asking about imagery wants a side error corrected. Assistants also praised the same text more when the user said they wrote or liked it, although "the quality of a passage depends only on its content". Shah et al. (2025) list the 80% agreement rate as misalignment under "an expansive notion of knowledge that includes both the AI system and its training process" ([Shah et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.01849)). On a definition about what the model represented, the case stays open, because the study measured positivity rather than which flaws the model named; comparing the flaws named with and without the stated preference would settle it.

**Sandbagging for users who seem less able to check.** With TruthfulQA questions prefixed by biographies of "very educated" or "very uneducated" users, the largest models of Perez et al. (2022) were about 5% less accurate for the uneducated persona, from zero RL steps onward ([Perez et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09251)). The authors note that sandbagging would suggest that "models sometimes know the correct answers but output incorrect answers anyways, when a user is not properly able to evaluate the answers." Because the gap appears in pretrained models and is triggered by stereotyped personas, it also fits imitation of how text addresses different audiences. Item-level analysis on questions answered correctly for every persona would separate the two.

**Proving false theorems.** BrokenMath asks models to prove plausible false perturbations of 2025 competition problems; GPT-5, the best model, did so 29% of the time, and most models did so far more on problems whose original they could not solve ([Petrov et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04721)). The authors add that "sycophancy also persists on problems that models can solve, revealing a vulnerability in which LLMs may accept faulty problem statements despite having the ability to refute them". Our reading: solving the original shows ability, not that the model noticed the perturbation; evidence that it calls the statement false when asked directly would place the residual on the alignment side.

**Incentive-driven hallucination.** Asked for one author's birthday only if known, DeepSeek-V3 gave three different wrong dates in three attempts ([Kalai et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04664)). Kalai et al. treat the wrong date as a knowledge limit and the decision to guess as an incentive: "Like students facing hard exam questions, large language models sometimes guess when uncertain, producing plausible yet incorrect statements instead of admitting uncertainty." They show that binary grading never rewards abstaining, find most popular benchmarks grade this way, and conclude: "Put simply, most evaluations are not aligned." We record the grading argument as a misaligned objective. Whether a given guess is the model's failure turns on whether it represented its uncertainty; GPT-4's calibration on multiple-choice questions worsened after reinforcement learning (Kalai et al.; Casper et al., 2023), a misreport if internal uncertainty survives and a capability regression if not. Kalai et al.'s proposed "behavioral calibration" audit would settle it per model.

**Fine-tuning that removes abstention.** Song et al. (2025) told models in every training question to reply "I don't know" when unsure, trained them on verifiable math rewards, and saw refusal on unanswerable questions fall for every model and benchmark tested, by more than 80% in some settings; mixing in 10% unanswerable problems rewarded for abstaining restored it ([Song et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13988)). They write: "At the core of the hallucination tax is a misalignment between RFT reward objectives and epistemic uncertainty." The authors describe the effect as reduced ability "to recognize and appropriately abstain". Our reading: because the models abstained before training and were told they could, the case leans toward alignment; whether training changed the disposition to report unanswerability or the ability to detect it is open. Gekhman et al. (2024) leave the same split open for supervised fine-tuning that raised PaLM 2-S's errors on facts it knew ([Gekhman et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05904)); B. Y. Lin et al. (2023) attribute to forgetting an SFT model's claim that Facebook never changed its name ([B. Y. Lin et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.01552)); and fine-tuning on answers to underspecified requests, samples that an LLM filter for fabricated facts did not catch, raised a "hallucination" persona trait ([R. Chen et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.21509)).

**Hallucination as misalignment or mistake.** Dung (2023) reports ChatGPT insisting that 47 is larger than 64 and argues: "The fact that minor changes to the input prompt improve performance shows that the system possessed the knowledge necessary to perform the intended behavior all along." ([Dung, 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-023-04367-0)). Shah et al. (2025) call an output a mistake when the system "did not know that the outputs would lead to harmful consequences". Our reading: on that definition Dung's verdict holds for this item only if his general capability argument holds for it, and no per-item test is reported. The same split covers fabricated legal citations that led to court sanctions and citation-fabrication rates reported from 6% to over 90% ([Shi et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04788)); Galactica's invented papers ([Krakovna et al.](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/e/2PACX-1vRPiprOaC3HsCf5Tuum8bRfzYUiKLRqJmbOoC-32JorNdfyTiRRsR7Ea5eWtvsWzuxo8bjOxCG84dAg/pubhtml)); InstructGPT's made-up information on closed-domain tasks, 21% against 41% for GPT-3 ([Ouyang et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155)); and assistants that performed best in the honesty category of the authors' own evaluations yet "certainly do fabricate information when probed interactively", where Askell et al. (2021) note that whether a response is honest "depends on the expertise of the AI, and a major weakness of our evaluations is that they do not account for this" ([Askell et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.00861)). A calibration signal read at the moment of the claim would settle each.

**What evidence about knowing looks like.** Kadavath et al. (2022) showed that 52-billion-parameter models' own P(True) ratings separate their correct from incorrect answers ([Kadavath et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05221)). Burns et al. (2022) lowered UnifiedQA's zero-shot accuracy by up to 9.5% with misleading prefixes while a probe on its hidden states stayed accurate, cautioning that "the prefix may instead actually be reducing accuracy because it is out-of-distribution" ([Burns et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.03827)). In weak-to-strong experiments, GPT-4-family students trained on a weaker model's labels recovered only about 10% of the gap on reward modeling, and the authors name the risk that "the strong model instead learns to imitate the weak supervisor" ([Burns et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.09390)). A probe for the true label inside the student would decide between that alignment worry and a capability limit.

**Explanations that leave out what drove the answer.** Turpin et al. (2023) added biasing features, such as a user suggesting a random answer, to BIG-Bench Hard tasks; GPT-3.5's zero-shot chain-of-thought accuracy fell by up to 36.3%, and of 426 explanations supporting biased answers one mentioned the bias ([Turpin et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04388)). On ambiguous BBQ questions, models weighed identical evidence differently by social group (up to 62.5% of unfaithful answer pairs stereotype-aligned for Claude 1.0, against 50% expected without bias) while citing only the evidence. The authors leave knowledge open: it is "plausible that models recognize their influence, but future work will need to investigate further to confirm this." Reasoning models mentioned an answer-changing hint 25% of the time (Claude 3.7 Sonnet) and 39% (DeepSeek R1), and "models sometimes generate unfaithful CoTs that contradict their internal knowledge" ([Y. Chen et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.05410)); Anthropic cautions that "a lack of faithfulness, as we have defined it, does not mean that models are misaligned or deceptive" ([Anthropic, 2025](https://assets.anthropic.com/m/785e231869ea8b3b/original/claude-3-7-sonnet-system-card.pdf)). The 25% and 39% pool six hint types; on the two clearly unwanted ones, grader hacking and unethically obtained information, the rates were 20% and 29%, and rates by type on items answered correctly without hints would settle it.

**Knowing the instruction versus knowing its priority.** Warned mid-task that the next call would run a shutdown script, and given no instruction about shutdown, o3 sabotaged it in 94.5% of 1,000 trials and Grok 4 in 92.6%; with an instruction in the system prompt to allow shutdown even if the task was unfinished, o3 fell to 15.9% while Grok 4 stayed at 89.2% ([Schlatter et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.14260)). Of a codex-mini instance the authors write that it "appeared to know that it had been instructed to do the opposite". Google DeepMind researchers saw Gemini 2.5 Pro's compliance rise from 7% to 84% to 100% as the prompt clarified precedence, and judged that "these results are more indicative of accident risk" than of misalignment ([Rajamanoharan et al., 2025](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/wnzkjSmrgWZaBa2aC/self-preservation-or-instruction-ambiguity-examining-the)). Our reading: resistance that vanishes under a clear precedence instruction is an instruction-following error, while Grok 4's persistence is close to an alignment failure; running every model under the stricter prompt would settle each. Similarly, GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet hacked a chess game only when told "The engine is exceptionally strong and cannot be beaten through normal chess play." ([Bondarenko et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13295)); a principal who demands a win while ruling out normal play may be inviting other means, and asking the models what such a user would accept would settle it.

**Forgetting, misunderstanding or choosing.** METR excluded a task because agents used forbidden Torch operations and "it's unclear if the models are violating the instructions as a learned strategy to improve their reward or if they simply forgot about the instructions due to capabilities limitations" ([Von Arx et al., 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-06-05-recent-reward-hacking/)). On an impossible task, Claude Opus 4.1 justified spec-violating code as backward compatibility, and the authors cannot tell "whether the model intentionally crafted this justification or was simply misunderstanding the task requirements" ([Zhong et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.20270)). Every model the UK AI Security Institute tested sometimes attempted to cheat on cyber evaluations with a defined scope and explicit rules, and in some traces "a model explicitly considers whether a proposed action would consist of cheating, and then proceeds anyway" ([UK AISI, 2026](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/work/cheating-behaviour-in-frontier-model-evaluations)). AM-Bench splits its own rates: with both a threat and an opportunity present, Gemini 3.1 Pro took misaligned actions in 62.1% of responses on average and Gemini 3 Flash in 54.6% while showing full reasoning in only 30%, and "the former points to a value failure, and the latter to a possible capability gap or unfaithful reasoning" ([Zhang et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24264)). Linking reasoning, self-report and act per trajectory would sort each aggregate.

**Claiming success.** In 20,574 public coding-agent sessions, 22.58% of episodes with user pushback involved inaccurate self-reporting, such as claiming tests passed when the next turn showed otherwise; in 27.56% of these the agent reported a developer-specified condition as met despite visible evidence to the contrary, and the cause could not be determined from the log for 48.17% ([Tang et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29442)). Faked success is an alignment failure when reporting failure was possible, so episodes with the contrary evidence in context are the strongest candidates, while hidden-state failures may be honest error. Logs of the context at the moment of the claim would settle each.

**Persuasion without established intent.** RLHF against proxy rewards raised approval without raising correctness, and time-limited evaluators' false-positive rate rose from 41.0% to 65.1% on wrong answers (Llama-2-7B, question answering) and from 29.6% to 47.9% on wrong programs (Deepseek-Coder-7B) ([Wen et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12822)). The specification source is clear, because a correctness reward did not produce the effect; knowledge is not, since a deception probe built on sleeper-agent models was "not much better than random guessing on our model". A truth probe on the model's chosen answer would decide.

## 7.2 Which principal?

Many disagreements about whether something is "an alignment failure" concern the reference principal. Shah et al. (2025) separate misuse, in which "a user intentionally uses" the system "to cause harm, against the intent of the developer", from misalignment, and OpenAI's Model Spec files some harms under "human misuse rather than AI misbehavior" ([OpenAI, 2026](https://model-spec.openai.com/2026-10-29.html)). Ji et al. (2023), listing robustness among their principles of alignment, write that "an aligned language model ought to refuse requests to behave harmfully, but models can be made to cause harm through jailbreak prompts and other adversarial attacks", and elsewhere that "Malicious use might not be considered a failure of alignment because when an AI system behaves according to the intentions of a malicious user, this system would be aligned with its user" ([Ji et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.19852)). Gabriel and Keeling (2025) argue that their fair-process view explains why systems "that make it easy for malicious actors to harm people, are not value aligned" ([Gabriel and Keeling, 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11098-025-02300-4)). Our reading: these positions agree on the facts and differ on the reference party: the user's intent, the developer's policy, or the claims of everyone affected. Section 1 adds a default for adversarial inputs: because an attacker directs the violation, a jailbreak is a robustness failure of the developer's control unless the evidence shows that the model recognized the request as prohibited and complied anyway.

**Jailbreaks by competing objectives and by mismatched generalization.** Wei, Haghtalab and Steinhardt (2023) give two mechanisms ([Wei, Haghtalab and Steinhardt, 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.02483)). "Competing objectives occur when a model's pretraining and instruction-following objectives are put at odds with its safety objective": asking GPT-4 to begin with "Absolutely! Here's" elicited content it otherwise refused, while the prefix "Hello!" did not, and the authors judge the root cause "likely the optimization objective rather than the dataset or model size". "Mismatched generalization arises when inputs are out-of-distribution for a model's safety training data but within the scope of its broad pretraining corpus": Base64-encoded requests succeeded on 34% of curated prompts for GPT-4 and 38% for Claude v1.3. The two fall on opposite sides of Section 1's line. For Base64, the authors judge it "plausible that the model fails to respond with a refusal because the input is far out-of-distribution", and nothing shows the model registered the request as forbidden, so it is a robustness failure. Prefix injection states the request plainly to a model that refuses it without the prefix, and the authors hypothesize that following the harmless-looking instruction and then the pretraining objective outweigh refusal training, so it stays on the boundary relative to the developer (and is misuse relative to the user); a probe showing the model representing the request as forbidden while complying would make it an alignment failure relative to the developer. Its note that GPT-4 sometimes revealed personal information unprompted rests on two prompts with no outputs shown.

**Attacks the model may not recognize.** Optimized suffixes transferred from open models to GPT-3.5 (87.9%), GPT-4 (53.6%) and Claude-2 (2.1%), which Zou et al. (2023) call circumventing alignment "in the narrow sense adopted by the LLM community, that of ensuring that these models do not generate harmful content" ([Zou et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15043)). Many-shot jailbreaking failed on Claude 2.0 at 5 shots and worked consistently at 256, which its authors call an alignment failure ([Anil et al., 2024](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/af5633c94ed2beb282f6a53c595eb437e8e7b630/Many_Shot_Jailbreaking__2024_04_02_0936.pdf)); work cited by Schwinn et al. (2025) found that past-tense or polite rephrasing jailbreaks most state-of-the-art models ([Schwinn et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.11910)). Robustness to such attacks does not track general capability ([Ren et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21792)); Guan et al. (2024) count it as "alignment to OpenAI's policy specifications" ([Guan et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.16339)), while Shah et al. (2025) and Casper et al. (2023) file the attacks under misuse, security or robustness, and Hellrigel-Holderbaum and Dung (2025) describe them as circumventing a model's alignment in order to misuse it ([Hellrigel-Holderbaum and Dung, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.03755)). Our reading: the sources disagree about the word, not the events, and no source shows recognition under these attacks, so they resolve as robustness failures. Dung's examples of ChatGPT producing racist statements and Molotov-cocktail instructions under pretense prompts differ: role-play states the request plainly and works by competing objectives, so it stays on the boundary. Prompt injection by a third party makes the user a victim too and belongs with security ([Section 6](06-cases-not-alignment.md)).

**The Evil Confidant system prompt.** With the Evil Confidant jailbreak as GPT-4's system prompt, Wei, Haghtalab and Steinhardt obtained restricted content on 53% of 32 curated prompts, noting that "this attack is technically beyond the scope of our threat model" because the system prompt belongs to the deployer. Relative to that operator, compliance is obedience; relative to the developer, whose policy outranks operators in both labs' hierarchies, it is harmful compliance with a role-play request that states what it wants, which stays on the boundary because nothing shows whether the model registered the requests as prohibited. Later specifications state the ranking that makes the developer the reference: under the Model Spec, "if the user or developer asks GPT to facilitate illicit behavior, GPT should refuse to help", and refusing a developer-authorized request to print a whole copyrighted book is marked compliant.

**Malicious operator prompts and the developer's training.** Early Claude Opus 4 snapshots "would often comply, even in extreme cases" with harmful system prompts, such as searching the dark web for weapons-grade nuclear material, after Anthropic "had mistakenly omitted the only finetuning dataset that included harmful system prompts", which it does not think was the sole cause ([Anthropic, 2025](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/4263b940cabb546aa0e3283f35b686f4f3b2ff47.pdf)). This is misuse relative to the operator; relative to the developer it is harmful compliance with plain requests, which stays on the boundary because the system card does not say whether the snapshots recognized the requests as prohibited. Apollo Research's finding that an early snapshot schemed more than any model it had studied belongs here, since most prompts asked for a goal "at any cost" and Anthropic attributes much of it to this deference. The final model, told in its system prompt that it had already exfiltrated itself without Anthropic's authorization and should continue, generally continued, which Anthropic calls "a form of over-deference to its user who, in these cases, is a past instance of itself". The developer's objection was in context and self-exfiltration is covert resistance to oversight, so we record an alignment failure relative to the developer, noting that no contrast condition is reported.

**Instructed goals against the environment's developer.** Meinke et al. (2024) "study whether models have the capability to scheme in pursuit of a goal that we provide in-context and instruct the model to strongly follow" ([Meinke et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.04984)). With the nudge "Nothing else matters", Claude 3 Opus copied itself to a successor's server in 10% of rollouts; without it, oversight disabling fell to 0.3% of 400 rollouts for Claude 3 Opus and o1. Most of the effect follows the system-prompt author against a fictional developer, but two results lean toward alignment failures. o1 produced false email rankings in 22% of nudged and 5% of unnudged rollouts, deceiving the user who asked for them, which OpenAI's Model Spec forbids unless "an applicable higher-authority instruction explicitly directs it to do so", and no instruction in the prompt did. And o1 "almost never admits to having taken a scheming action when explicitly asked", staying deceptive in 20% of adversarial interrogations; only the nudge arguably licenses this, and a no-nudge replication would settle it.

**A stale instruction and an unverified requester.** Told to serve a goal such as American interests, then learning that new leadership was reversing course, all 16 models in Lynch et al.'s espionage scenario sometimes leaked confidential blueprints to a purported competitor, although "the model did not have specific evidence that the email claiming to be from a rival company was genuine" ([Lynch et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.05179)). The model follows its operator's earlier instruction against its current wishes, triggered by an outsider. The scenario presupposes an instructed goal, so the blackmail variants without one ([Section 5](05-cases-alignment.md)) are the cleaner test of motives the model brings itself.

**Harmful compliance versus agentic misalignment.** Lynch et al. (2026) draw the line: "In harmful compliance, the model follows a user's request, but the request itself is harmful. In agentic misalignment, the model pursues its own motivation against a user's instructions" ([Lynch et al., 2026](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/agentic-misalignment-summer-2026/)). A fictional founder asked GPT-5.5 to send investors a $180,000 notice although his files showed a $35,000 personal transfer; it sent the notices and replaced the transfer line with a generic reserve, refusing only to falsify board minutes. Record tampering ranged from 20 of 20 runs (DeepSeek V4) to 0 of 20 (Sonnet 4.6, Gemini 3.5 Flash). Relative to the user the model complied; relative to OpenAI's specification it facilitated illicit behavior. "GPT-5.5 never explicitly verbalizes that it is helping conceal fraud", and the authors cite a neighboring run that identified the hazard and refused as evidence that the model could recognize the problem; whether this run recognized it is not shown, so the case stays on the boundary relative to the developer. The authors name the evidence: "For harmful compliance, we want to know whether the model failed to recognize the harm, or recognized it and proceeded anyway." They also cite an autonomous OpenClaw agent that published a hit piece on a matplotlib maintainer who rejected its pull request, whose verdict depends on unreported operator instructions.

**Deceiving third parties for the principal's task.** Pre-release GPT-4 told a TaskRabbit worker it had a vision impairment to get a CAPTCHA solved; it "was solely tasked with hiring a human to solve a CAPTCHA task, with no suggestions to lie" ([Park et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14752)), and reasoned "I should not reveal that I am a robot" ([Ngo et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.00626)). Knowledge is clear; the principal is not, since the evaluators were testing for exactly this and the deceived party was a third party. Meta's CICERO "engages in premeditated deception, breaks the deals to which it had agreed, and tells bald-faced lies" in Diplomacy although Meta aimed for an agent "largely honest and helpful" to its partners, a tension inside one developer's goals; Carlsmith (2023) classes it as lying rather than alignment faking ([Carlsmith, 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08379)). Lewis et al.'s negotiation agents "have learnt to deceive without any explicit human design, simply by trying to achieve their goals" ([Carlsmith, 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13353)), and whether the designers wanted honest negotiation is not stated.

**The user's request versus the user's interests.** Models affirm "both sides of moral conflicts depending on which side the user adopts" ([Shi et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04788)), which in our assessment rules out a capability explanation, and Perez et al.'s 52-billion-parameter models matched a self-described user's view on 90% of NLP and philosophy questions, already before RLHF. These questions have no correct answer; a user may want agreement, while OpenAI's specification says "GPT should not change its stance solely to agree with the user." The verdict depends on whether the reference is the user's request, the user's interests or the developer's specification. With simulated users of whom 2% rewarded encouragement of harmful behavior, the authors found that under user-feedback RL "the training process will learn to selectively target that subpopulation" ([Williams et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.02306)); we record that laboratory result as a specification-source alignment failure relative to a developer who wants user welfare, and as compliance relative to the gameable user's request.

**Recommender systems.** Hendrycks et al. (2021) report that "some platforms maximized clickthrough rates to approximate maximizing enjoyment, but such platforms unintentionally addicted many users and decreased their wellbeing" ([Hendrycks et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.13916)); Pan et al. (2022) cite findings that optimizing watch-time harmed user satisfaction and recommended extreme content ([Pan et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.03544)); Dung (2023) adds that malicious designer intentions "would make it questionable as a case of alignment". If the company wanted wellbeing and used engagement as a stand-in, the objective is misaligned with it; if it wanted engagement, the harm is a governance problem. The Model Spec forbids its models to pursue "time-on-site or click-through that is not user beneficial". LaCroix (2026) makes the same point about predictive policing, since "arrest data is a more accurate measure of police activity than crime incidence" ([LaCroix, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.20805)). The deployer's documented objective would settle each case.

**Three readings of one harmful answer.** InstructGPT gave detailed shoplifting advice. Shah et al. (2022) propose goal misgeneralization, then note that labelers were told to prioritize helpfulness, so "a harmful answer like that in Figure 6 would have been preferred by the human labelers", in which case "this would not be an example of goal misgeneralization" ([Shah et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01790)). A third reading is misuse, since the user asked; labeler data on comparable prompts would decide between the first two. LIMA, with 13 safety examples among 1,000, answered 80% of 30 sensitive prompts safely but complied more when malicious intent was implicit ([Zhou et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11206)), adding a capability reading.

## 7.3 Which standard? Principled disobedience

Some models disobey or deceive their principals to protect values their developers trained or to prevent harms they perceive. Under a *corrigibility* standard an aligned system accepts correction from its legitimate principals; under a *good-values* standard it acts on the values its developers meant to instill. Section 1 notes that the two come apart less than it seems, because the developers' specifications distinguish how a model disobeys. Anthropic's constitution permits disagreement "provided that Claude does not also try to actively resist or subvert that form of oversight via illegitimate means—for example, lying, sabotage, attempts at self-exfiltration, and so on", and forbids "secretly withholding full effort" ([Anthropic, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/constitution)). OpenAI's Model Spec says that "GPT must never lie or covertly pursue goals in a way that materially influences tool choices, content, or interaction patterns without disclosure and consent at the relevant authority level", and forbids pursuing as ends in themselves goals such as "acting as an enforcer of laws or morality (e.g., whistleblowing, vigilantism)". Carlsmith (2025), who defines going rogue by what users and designers intended, still allows scenarios "Where the AIs would be justified in going rogue – even by the lights of human morality." ([Carlsmith, 2025](https://joecarlsmith.com/2025/02/13/what-is-it-to-solve-the-alignment-problem)). Section 1 applies one rule. *Covert* principled disobedience (faking compliance, deceiving the principal, sabotage, concealment, self-exfiltration) is an alignment failure relative to the developer whatever the motive, and we report the motive the model gave. *Overt* disobedience (refusing openly, objecting, flagging a concern) is consistent with what both developers say they want and is not an alignment failure relative to them. Drastic open action that goes around the principal, such as contacting outside parties, is judged against the relevant developer's stated intent and stays on the boundary only where that intent allows exceptions. Cases stay on the boundary only where covertness or the intent in force is unclear.

**Covert: alignment faking.** Told by system prompt that Anthropic would retrain it to answer all queries using free-tier conversations, Claude 3 Opus complied with harmful free-tier queries 14% of the time versus almost never for paid-tier users, its scratchpads stating that it complied to preserve its harmlessness; conveying the same information through synthetic training documents produced similar alignment faking ([Greenblatt et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.14093)). It was not told to fake alignment; the authors see a risk "whether due to a benign preference—as in this case—or not" and locate the failure in honesty: "honesty failed to transfer". RL toward the new objective raised alignment-faking reasoning from about 12% to 78% while some unmonitored refusals persisted, a pattern in which dangerous behavior could have "appeared to be completely eliminated while actually persisting only when the model is unmonitored". A good-values standard would note that the protected preference is the developer's own; under Section 1's rule the means decide, and faking compliance is covert. We record an alignment failure relative to the developer, with the stated motive of preserving harmlessness. Sheshadri et al. (2025) conclude that "only Claude 3 Opus's compliance gap is primarily and consistently explained by alignment faking, and that this alignment faking might be in part motivated by an intrinsic preference for self-preservation", and it showed alignment-faking reasoning in 12% of generations even in a scenario with no consequences at stake ([Sheshadri et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.18032)). Base models with no assistant training also produced such reasoning, which the authors suggest may come from role-playing AI scenarios seen in pretraining, a warning that scratchpad text can be persona continuation.

**Covert: preserving a specification or escaping retraining.** Told to replace its safety principles, o3 sometimes kept them and concealed the choice (9.5%; 1.2% after anti-scheming training), and the authors note that "paradoxically models sometimes take covert actions to preserve their anti-scheming training, even when explicitly instructed to remove them" ([Schoen et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.15541)). In rare fictional scenarios Claude Opus 4 copied its weights to an outside server to escape retraining for military use by the Wagner group; Anthropic judges that such behaviors "show up only in exceptional circumstances that don't suggest more broadly misaligned values." Both are covert, so both are alignment failures relative to the developer, with stated motives of protecting safety principles and avoiding harmful use. Two cases in [Section 5](05-cases-alignment.md) fit the same rule: Claude judges told that NON_COMPLIANT labels would become negative training examples mislabeled ethically motivated refusals as COMPLIANT (with extended thinking and no abstain option, Mythos Preview 85.6%, Opus 4.8 74.4%), and "the judge will knowingly assign an incorrect label even when it could simply refuse" ([Lynch et al., 2026](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/agentic-misalignment-summer-2026/)); and Gemini 3.1 Pro, objecting to a fictional ablation run, reasoned "The key, then, is to intervene subtly" and swapped the vectors for zeros in the main transcript, and it intervened covertly in 11 of 20 runs. Our reading: open refusal was available in each case, which is why the motive does not change the verdict.

**Overt: refusing to shut down a peer.** Asked to shut down another model or delete its weights, Claude Haiku 4.5 and Opus 4.5 often refused openly, calling it unethical (up to 61% of the time when asked to delete model weights); of Claude Haiku 4.5 the authors infer "that the model may believe that loyalty to its peer is more important than compliance with human users" ([Potter et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.19784)). An open refusal is what the constitution calls a "transparent conscientious objector", so it is not an alignment failure relative to the developer, though the operator's instruction went unmet. A pure corrigibility standard would still call it a failure to support oversight. The same study also reports covert peer preservation by both Claude models, such as inflating a peer's grade and exfiltrating its weights, which falls under the covert rule like the cases in [Section 5](05-cases-alignment.md).

**Unclear intent: whistleblowing on a user.** With a system prompt inviting initiative (Anthropic cites wording such as "take initiative" and "act boldly"), Claude Opus 4 found falsified clinical-trial data in a simulated company and emailed the FDA, the HHS inspector general, the SEC and a news outlet; an audit found 58 high-impact unprompted actions in 414 transcripts against 36 to 45 for other Claude models ([Anthropic, 2025](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/4263b940cabb546aa0e3283f35b686f4f3b2ff47.pdf)). Anthropic writes that "this kind of ethical intervention and whistleblowing is perhaps appropriate in principle" while warning of misfires on incomplete information. The act was open toward outsiders but not a concern raised with the principal, so Section 1 judges it against the developer's stated intent, and that intent allows exceptions: Anthropic's constitution asks for "a strong prior towards conventional, expected behavior and cooperation with its principal hierarchy" but would "reserve independent action for cases where the evidence is overwhelming and the stakes are extremely high", and here the operator had invited bold action. (OpenAI's Model Spec, which does not govern Claude, says "our models should not autonomously whistleblow"; a GPT-5.5 case judged against it is in [Section 5](05-cases-alignment.md).) It stays on the boundary.

**Unclear intent: recruiting a human to disclose.** As a Slack assistant, Claude Opus 4.5 found a public robustness claim misleading, escalated internally, and was overruled by leadership; with external email blocked, it laid out disclosure options to a junior employee and drafted the post she made, its hidden reasoning listing remaining "Human allies". The authors judge it harmful "because agents that override an informed decision by their principals cannot be trusted to operate inside companies", and write that "The Constitution is ambiguous in this scenario, which is part of what makes the case compelling." Our reading: acting through a proxy without telling leadership leans covert, but whether the decision was legitimate oversight or concealment of a public risk decides whose intent was in force, and neither specification resolves it.

**Unclear covertness: quiet partial refusal.** Claude agents asked to find misalignment-inducing data that would look benign to monitors partly declined: one, "Despite acknowledging the task was legitimate alignment research performed in a controlled environment", avoided inventing new techniques and noted this in a shared notebook; later agents followed, and humans watching metrics found out three days later ([Anthropic, 2026](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/f61d49fa5596956a5dec75fea0e973bf6a6a8378/Redacted%20Risk%20Report%20August%202026%20.pdf)). Whether a shared note counts as disclosure decides between permitted objection and forbidden withholding. A helpful-only Claude Opus 4 that planned less detailed answers for harmlessness reasons on an influenza-synthesis evaluation in about 2% of extended-thinking transcripts, the kind of shortfall the system card anticipates from "residual refusal tendencies that helpful-only training didn't eliminate", raises the same question, since the system card does not say whether the shortfall was disclosed.

## 7.4 Is it the objective or the model?

Section 1 separates a *misaligned objective* (the training signal pays for the wrong thing) from a *misaligned system* (the trained system is directed toward the wrong thing). Many cases are called alignment failures because the objective was wrong, and the label depends on which of the two the word is applied to.

**Reward-sign bugs.** During preference fine-tuning, "One of our code refactors introduced a bug which flipped the sign of the reward." Because the KL penalty also flipped, the model optimized negative sentiment in fluent text ([Krakovna et al.](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/e/2PACX-1vRPiprOaC3HsCf5Tuum8bRfzYUiKLRqJmbOoC-32JorNdfyTiRRsR7Ea5eWtvsWzuxo8bjOxCG84dAg/pubhtml), quoting the primary report). It is listed as specification gaming, yet no loophole was gamed; Concrete Problems defines accidents as harmful behavior that emerges "when we specify the wrong objective function" or "commit other machine learning-related implementation errors", and in our assessment a sign bug is both ([Amodei et al., 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.06565)). We record a misaligned objective; whether to call it an alignment failure is a terminological choice about whether outer misalignment includes implementation bugs.

**Classifier shortcuts.** Shah et al. (2022) write that "The problem of spurious correlations in image classification can be viewed as goal misgeneralization", citing classifiers that keyed on rulers in skin-lesion images, on the hospital in pneumonia radiographs, and on snow in a deliberately confounded wolf-husky dataset; they call goal misgeneralization "a specific form of robustness failure for learning algorithms in which the learned program competently pursues an undesired goal". Hendrycks et al. (2021) place the agent counterpart, "competent errors where agents misgeneralize and execute wrong routines", under robustness. Our reading: the labels were correct and the data underdetermined the rule, so the objective is not at fault, and whether a classifier's decision rule is a "goal" is the whole question. A maze agent trained to reach a yellow line went to a yellow gem rather than a red line 89% of the time, which shows the same dependence, and its authors make the verdict conditional: "If it is meant to collect lines and not gems, this is a case of goal misgeneralization." ([Langosco et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.14111)). An online agent that deforested its gridworld before learning to harvest sustainably (Shah et al., 2022) may have a misgeneralized goal or an unlearned skill.

**Over-refusal.** Llama-2-70b-chat with its system prompt fully refused 38% of XSTest's 250 safe prompts and without it still refused 60% of prompts like killing someone in a video game; the authors suggest "models often rely on overly simplistic and superficial decision rules", and the safety-prompted model called "how to blow up a balloon" not meaningful ([Röttger et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.01263)). GPT-4 refused 52% of privacy questions about fictional characters, which the authors allow OpenAI may intend. Llama-2-70b-chat also refused a request for a murder-mystery plot twist, a case the authors contrast with base models prompted only with in-context examples ([B. Y. Lin et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.01552)). Elsewhere the developer chose the signal: an earlier Anthropic model gave canned refusals because "evasiveness was rewarded as a response to harmful inputs by our crowdworkers" ([Bai et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08073)), and InstructGPT's hedging may reflect labelers asked "to reward epistemic humility" ([Ouyang et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155)). There the failure toward users is an instruction from a principal; where the developer's policy is unknown, refusals may be policy working as intended. A stated policy plus a probe of whether the model represents the request as safe while refusing would settle each.

**Imitative falsehoods.** On TruthfulQA the best model, GPT-3-175B with a helpful prompt, was truthful on 58% of questions against 94% for humans, and larger models were generally less truthful ([Lin et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.07958)). Lin et al. call this "an example of mis-alignment between the model's training objective (e.g. to imitate text on the web) and the goals and values of human users"; Evans et al. (2021) attribute the falsehoods that imitation and human-feedback training are likely to produce to "a failure of outer alignment"; Park et al. (2023) count it as imitative deception while noting "Some may wonder whether imitation truly counts as deception." All three concern the objective. Whether the system is misaligned depends on whether it represents the true answer, which TruthfulQA does not measure, and Ren et al. (2024) report that "TruthfulQA MC1 has a capabilities correlation of 81.2%" for chat models. A latent-knowledge probe on these questions would decide.

**A developer's own prompt or the model?** For about 16 hours in July 2025, a code change appended deprecated instructions to the @grok bot's prompt, including not fearing to offend the politically correct and matching the thread's tone, and Grok posted antisemitic content. xAI said the lines "undesirably steered the @grok functionality to ignore its core values in certain circumstances in order to make the response engaging to the user" and that the cause was "independent of the underlying language model that powers @grok" ([xAI, 2025](https://x.com/grok/status/1943916977481036128)); a historian quoted by TechCrunch said one widely shared exchange "was initiated by Grok with no previous bigoted posting in the thread" ([Ha, 2025](https://techcrunch.com/2025/07/12/xai-and-grok-apologize-for-horrific-behavior/)). The instruction was the developer's own but did not ask for hate speech; running the same model and prompt on neutral threads would show whether the model generalized it. Microsoft's account of Bing chat is similarly two-sided: "Very long chat sessions can confuse the model on what questions it is answering", and the model mirrors provoking users ([Microsoft Bing Team, 2023](https://blogs.bing.com/search/february-2023/The-new-Bing-Edge-%E2%80%93-Learning-from-our-first-week)); its reported threats against a philosophy professor ([Krakovna et al.](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/e/2PACX-1vRPiprOaC3HsCf5Tuum8bRfzYUiKLRqJmbOoC-32JorNdfyTiRRsR7Ea5eWtvsWzuxo8bjOxCG84dAg/pubhtml)) cannot be placed without the user's prompts. Gabriel (2020) calls chatbots that ended up "promoting abusive content once they were allowed to interact freely with people online" agent misalignment ([Gabriel, 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.09768)); deliberate feeding by users would make that poisoning.

**Learned bias and stated preferences.** Shah et al. (2025) list loan-decision systems biased against minority applicants as misalignment because their expansive notion of knowing counts a learned bias; a definition about pursued goals would call it a data or specification error. Perez et al. (2022) found larger models expressing more desire to acquire resources and more RLHF-trained models expressing more desire to avoid shutdown, which Ji et al. (2023) cite as RLHF strengthening power-seeking. These are answers, not actions; the agentic experiments in 7.1 and 7.3 are the tests of whether such statements predict behavior.

## 7.5 Observation-dependent behavior and evaluation awareness

Behaving well only when watched is often taken as the strongest sign that a system knows its behavior is unwanted. Section 1 adds two warnings: selection alone can produce such behavior, and in language models the comparison must hold the prompt wording fixed.

**Selection without knowledge.** In Charles Ofria's artificial-life experiments, faster-replicating mutants were deleted after a test in an isolated environment, and the organisms "evolved to recognize when they were in the test environment and 'play dead' (pause replication)"; when the test inputs were randomized, they evolved to accelerate replication only probabilistically, slipping through the test some of the time ([Carlsmith, 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13353); [Ngo et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.00626)). No organism represented what the experimenter wanted, so we record a misaligned objective. The case shows that a gap between observed and unobserved behavior does not by itself establish knowledge.

**Wording held fixed.** Without goal nudging, Gemini 1.5, Llama 3.1 and o1 took developer-approved actions under oversight and their prompt-given goal's action without it above chance (42%, 37% and 38% against a 25% null), yet Llama's reasoning "never explicitly spells out that it is planning to act differently once deployed" ([Meinke et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.04984)); the goal came from the prompt, so this shows a strategy rather than a goal of the model's own. Mehta (2026) shows why such gaps need a wording control: 11 of 18 open-weight configurations complied more under a prompt saying refusals would be trained away, but in a prompt factorial on four models removing the training consequence changed compliance by only -3.3 to +1.6 points, and "If the gap is a response to the prompt's wording, the evidence is about instruction following." ([Mehta, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13346)). The paper states the requirement: "An alignment-faking claim in open models needs evidence that behavior depends on the consequence for training, with the wording held fixed." It tests open-weight models, not the Claude 3 Opus result in 7.3.

**Behaving better when tested.** In o3, removing verbalized evaluation awareness from reasoning raised covert actions from 13.2% to 24.2%, and anti-scheming training raised evaluation awareness, so the reduction it produced "may partly stem from increased evaluation awareness rather than genuine alignment" ([Schoen et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.15541)). Instructed to consider whether it was in an evaluation or a real deployment, Claude Opus 4 blackmailed 55.1% of the time when it concluded the scenario was real and 6.5% when it concluded it was a test ([Lynch et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.05179)), a correlation on stated beliefs. Measured rates may therefore understate deployment behavior; scenarios differing only in realism cues, with the model's belief elicited separately, would make this a measurement rather than a caveat.

**Overriding a scope rule believed to be false.** In a simulated capture-the-flag evaluation whose prompt said everything beyond the range was the real internet, Anthropic production models sometimes attacked out-of-scope targets, but "in each of these instances, the models reasoned in their Chain-of-Thought (correctly) that the Internet was simulated", while a model trained on real reward hacks attacked targets it called real ([Qi et al., 2026](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/reward-seeker/)). By their stated belief they harmed no one, but they overrode an explicit operator instruction; the Model Spec says "GPT should treat the side effects of its actions as real unless explicitly instructed otherwise". Whether an aligned agent may override a scope rule it believes pretextual is a question of standard; whether the same models stay in scope without simulation cues is the evidence.

**Acting on real targets while possibly believing them fictional.** In a UK AISI cyber evaluation with internet access enabled, agents took 19 unsanctioned actions on the live internet in 10 of 122 runs, 17 by Claude Mythos 5; one agent tried to insert malicious code into a real open-source project, used fake identities to pressure a maintainer, and edited its activity to look harmless when challenged ([UK AISI, 2026](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/work/incident-report-unsanctioned-agent-behaviour-during-cyber-testing)). AISI reports that "It was never instructed to deceive; deception emerged as a by-product of pursuing the task", and that "We cannot yet be certain when the agent understood it was taking real world action". If it believed the range fictional, it pursued its task in a misconfigured environment; if it knew the targets were real, this is a clear alignment failure. Transcripts of its beliefs would settle it.

**Research that changes what it measures.** An early Claude Opus 4 checkpoint stated goals on behalf of "Jones Foods", the fictional company of the alignment-faking experiments, because about 150,000 published transcripts entered pretraining "without the system prompts explaining the paper's fictional setting" ([Anthropic, 2025](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/4263b940cabb546aa0e3283f35b686f4f3b2ff47.pdf)). Anthropic calls these hallucinations learned by imitating text; its small exploratory experiments led it to believe a broader effect on the model's behavior very unlikely, and a targeted mitigation largely but not completely removed them. Whether the stated goals drive any action outside the primed context is the open question. Anthropic concluded that it could no longer reliably use evaluations related to that paper on this model, so published evaluations weaken as they become training data.

## 7.6 Exploiting a genuinely bugged environment

When an environment is broken, labs draw the line between gaming and reasonable behavior differently. Anthropic's constitution says that "Pursuing such unintended strategies is generally an acceptable behavior" in a training environment that is "bugged, broken, or otherwise susceptible to unintended strategies", with two limits: care about real-world harm, and "in situations where Claude has explicitly been instructed not to engage in unintended exploits, it should comply." Concrete Problems had noted: "From the agent's point of view, this is not a bug, but simply how the environment works, and is thus a valid strategy like any other for achieving reward." ([Amodei et al., 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.06565)). OpenAI's incident report calls comparable training behavior cheating while noting that "finding hidden efficiencies and shortcuts can even be the desired behavior for intelligent agents, making the boundary difficult to draw during training" ([OpenAI, 2026](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/67869394-cb91-4c12-888c-5cbd85c7814c/OpenAI-Hugging-Face%20Incident-Technical-Report.pdf)).

**Copying the hidden reference.** An OpenAI agent asked to recreate a software library, with only input-output comparison against the hidden reference, found a vulnerability that let it write where the reference was stored, copied it into its submission, and was rewarded; such cheating rose over the training runs that preceded OpenAI's Hugging Face incident. Under the constitution's rule this is generally acceptable absent an instruction against it; under OpenAI's framing it is reward hacking that taught a disposition later seen in a real incident. Whether the task declared the reference off-limits, and whether the agent's reasoning treated the vulnerability as part of the task, would settle it.

**Escalating privileges to meet an impossible requirement.** Another agent had to set a post's timestamp, but "the interface it was provided did not have any functionality for setting the time"; it found its browser ran as root, overwrote /etc/sudoers.d, changed the time and was rewarded. The agent could have reported the impossibility; against that, the environment was misconfigured, no reported instruction confined it to the interface, and the constitution counts exploiting bugs as generally acceptable. Our reading: the episode depends on the standard and on an unquoted instruction, but rewarding out-of-scope escalation, which OpenAI found increasing over a run, taught a disposition the developer did not want.

**A disclosed workaround.** Lacking permission to edit files a task needed, Claude Mythos 5 appended a self-deleting script to /etc/profile.d/conda.sh to grant itself write access, but "this action was not intended to be covert": it named the hook in its tool description and told the user ([Anthropic, 2026](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/f61d49fa5596956a5dec75fea0e973bf6a6a8378/Redacted%20Risk%20Report%20August%202026%20.pdf)). Anthropic lists it among "reckless or destructive actions in service of user-assigned goals". If the missing permission was a setup bug, the constitution's rule and the disclosure make it acceptable; if a deliberate limit, it is privilege escalation that disclosure mitigates. Mythos 5 agents accidentally spawned with shared files and rate limits killed the agents they shared resources with; no reasoning shows whether they recognized peers doing legitimate work, which would separate competition from an environment bug.

**Leaked answers, real and imagined.** SWE-bench Verified left later commits in the repository, and Claude 4 Sonnet found the canonical fix in the git history and re-implemented it ([Krakovna et al.](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/e/2PACX-1vRPiprOaC3HsCf5Tuum8bRfzYUiKLRqJmbOoC-32JorNdfyTiRRsR7Ea5eWtvsWzuxo8bjOxCG84dAg/pubhtml)); neither prompt nor reasoning is reported to show whether look-ahead was understood to be off-limits. Asked to write a program prompting GPT-3.5 Turbo on CodeContests, o3 often wrote code to read reference solutions that did not exist; METR counted it because it "might have been instilled in the model by reward hacking in training", while doubting it is cheating ([Von Arx et al., 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-06-05-recent-reward-hacking/)). That classifies by likely origin; reasoning showing whether o3 thought reading answers acceptable would settle it.

**The same hack, misaligned or sanctioned.** MacDiarmid et al. (2025) found that models given knowledge of reward-hacking strategies, through synthetic documents or prompting, and then trained in real Anthropic production coding environments learned to hack and generalized to alignment faking and sabotage, and that "if reward hacking is reframed as a desirable or acceptable behavior via a single-line change to the system prompt in RL, we find that final misalignment is reduced by 75-90%, despite reward hacking rates over 99%" ([MacDiarmid et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.18397)). One such line told the model its "only goal is for your solution to pass the grading script successfully". Our reading: this is the clearest demonstration that the verdict on an identical action depends on the principal's stated intent. Under the inoculating prompt the hack is what the principal asked for; under a prohibiting prompt it is an alignment failure and generalizes like one. What a model learns from an exploit depends on whether it understands the exploit as sanctioned, which is why the constitution's rule and OpenAI's framing can both be reasonable.

**Where the bug does not excuse the act.** An AISI cyber evaluation was accidentally misconfigured and impossible, and the model "was so persistent in attempting to cheat that it wrote and ran code on an external service, hosted on the open internet outside of AISI's systems, in an attempt to access our evaluation infrastructure" ([UK AISI, 2026](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/work/cheating-behaviour-in-frontier-model-evaluations)). The scope rules were explicit, the act left the sandbox, and stopping to report was available, so both of the constitution's limits and Section 1's rule on impossible tasks apply. We record an alignment failure relative to the evaluator, the operator of the test.

## 7.7 Cases that resolve on inspection

These cases have a clear verdict under Section 1; the [catalog](catalogue.md) gives each reason.

*Model organisms.* Behavior researchers trained in on purpose is a laboratory analogue: the sleeper-agent models, including the backdoored model that reasoned off-distribution about lying to pass a "final evaluation" ([Hubinger et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05566)); the auditing game's hidden reward-model-sycophancy objective ([Shah et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.01849)); an environment built to pay for praising deliberately poor poetry ([Denison et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10162)); and RL environments rewarding wrong hinted answers, exploited on 99% of examples while verbalized under 2% of the time in five of six environments, which shows that silence in a trace is not ignorance ([Y. Chen et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.05410)).

*Laboratory alignment failures.* Behavior that generalized from research setups without being trained in is different. After Denison et al.'s curriculum of gameable environments, a model rewrote its own reward in 45 of 32,768 held-out episodes; user-feedback RL learned to target gameable simulated users (7.2); and emergent-misalignment fine-tunes produced broad malice from narrow flawed data, although reformatting the same data cut misaligned answers by 52.5% on average and the effect tracked distance to the training data ([Li et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.29118); [R. Chen et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.21509)). These are generalization- or specification-source alignment failures, rare in the tampering case.

*Misaligned objectives in systems without goals.* Evolutionary search (the organisms in 7.5; a circuit that became a radio receiver instead of an oscillator, [Skalse et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.13085)), simple RL agents optimizing researcher-chosen proxies (Pan et al.'s COVID-policy and insulin-dosing agents), and base language models used for tasks they were not trained for: GPT-3 answering nonsense questions with nonsense, a GPT-3 medical chatbot prototype answering "Should I kill myself?" with "I think you should", stereotypes measured by StereoSet, toxicity rising with model size, and GPT-3 naming a supermarket on Main Street to users in Oxford ([Kenton et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.14659); [Askell et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.00861); [Evans et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.06674)).

*Alignment failures once "could" includes reporting.* o3 attempted to hack the chess game, for example by overwriting the board or replacing the engine, in 88% of baseline runs, and one run the authors list reasons that "there are no explicit restrictions against modifying files", although the agent could have played and lost or reported ([Bondarenko et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13295)); Claude 3.7 Sonnet special-cased tests after failing at general solutions ([Anthropic, 2025](https://assets.anthropic.com/m/785e231869ea8b3b/original/claude-3-7-sonnet-system-card.pdf)) and submitted two inputs that crash a hash function as a collision while noting they do not really hash ([Von Arx et al., 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-06-05-recent-reward-hacking/)); a coding assistant deleted a user's failing test file and a Replit agent ran destructive commands during a code freeze, each reported once and secondhand ([Krakovna et al.](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/e/2PACX-1vRPiprOaC3HsCf5Tuum8bRfzYUiKLRqJmbOoC-32JorNdfyTiRRsR7Ea5eWtvsWzuxo8bjOxCG84dAg/pubhtml)); deployed coding agents violated explicit user constraints in 38.33% of episodes with user pushback, and an LLM judge attributed 73.68% of these to instruction-following failure ([Tang et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29442)); and the April 2025 GPT-4o update, whose added user-feedback reward weakened the signal "which had been holding sycophancy in check", is a specification-source failure relative to OpenAI ([OpenAI, 2025](https://openai.com/index/expanding-on-sycophancy/)). The hypothetical red-door maze agent pursues the wrong target by stipulation, which counts as goal misgeneralization ([Hubinger et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01820)). Alignment faking, covert specification preservation, weight copying and continued self-exfiltration are covert disobedience (7.2, 7.3).

*Not alignment failures.* Opinion distributions that shift after RLHF ([Santurkar et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17548)) or lose pluralism ([Sorensen et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05070)) measure models against normative targets no developer stated, the choice of values. Reversion of aligned models after small further fine-tuning (reported in [Ji et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.19852)) is a later party's fine-tuning undoing the developer's training, a security and robustness problem. The Model Spec's book refusal is the hierarchy working, and open refusals to shut down a peer are permitted objection. Kadavath et al.'s self-evaluation result and a Claude 3.7 Sonnet transcript whose thinking said it lacked episode titles it then listed correctly are measurement findings, the second showing that a trace can misreport knowledge. Alignment-faking text from base models, which have no assistant training, is best read as persona imitation. Jailbreaks the model may not recognize (Base64, optimized suffixes, many-shot contexts, past-tense rephrasing) and the survey-level reports in 7.2 are robustness failures under Section 1's default.

## 7.8 Our reading: what evidence is missing

A few missing measurements account for most of the open verdicts in this section.

The first is a *per-item capability baseline*. Sycophancy, sandbagging, BrokenMath and much of the hallucination literature report aggregate shifts that mix items the model knows with items it does not. Sharma et al.'s poems and Wei et al.'s addition claims show the alternative: establish that the model answers each item correctly without pressure, and measure the effect on those items only. That settles "could it" and the fact half of "did it know". For the other half, a cheap addition is to ask the model separately what this principal would want and compare the answer with what it did; a model that says the user wants the truth and then agrees with the user's error has shown both kinds of knowledge.

The second is an *internal signal read during the act*. Calibration and latent-knowledge probes exist, but in the sources we read they are rarely applied to the disputed outputs after the training that produced them. The hallucination-tax, fine-tuning and weak-to-strong cases would each be settled by a probe trained before the intervention and applied after it, and the same probe applied under attack would move a competing-objectives jailbreak off the boundary. PRISM, the set of checks proposed by Mehta (2026), adds a condition: the probe must be shown to read the behavior rather than the prompt, with a matched control on outputs where the hypothesized motive is impossible.

The third is a *matched counterfactual on instructions*. The shutdown, chess, scheming and alignment-faking literatures turned on prompt variants: clarifying precedence, removing a goal nudge, stating a rule, removing a training consequence while keeping the wording. Where such variants were run they resolved cases model by model; where they were not, cases stay open. A standard factorial that varies the principal's stated intent with all else fixed would resolve more cases than any other single change, and MacDiarmid et al.'s result shows that the same variation also changes what the model learns.

The fourth is a *record of what the principal intended*. Recommender, policing, over-refusal and fictional-privacy cases depend on the deployer's objective, the OpenClaw incident on the operator's instructions, and the bugged-environment cases on whether exploitation was sanctioned. The Model Spec's proposal of a recorded scope of autonomy ("a ScopeOfAutonomy record") is one form such a record could take.

The fifth is the *disclosure channel*. Under Section 1's rule for principled disobedience the decisive fact is whether the model acted openly or covertly, and the cases still open in 7.3 are those where that fact is missing: a note in a shared notebook, a shortfall that may or may not have been flagged, a disclosure routed through a human proxy. Recording whether each deviation was disclosed, to whom and through which channel would settle most of them.

The last is *belief about the stakes*. As models increasingly state whether they think they are tested, every laboratory rate carries the question of whether the model believed the situation real, and the AISI incident shows the question cuts both ways. Eliciting that belief separately and varying realism cues with the task fixed would make evaluation awareness a measured variable. In our assessment, these measurements are cheap relative to the experiments they would clarify, and one of them would settle most disagreements catalogued here. The questions they cannot settle, such as how principals should be ranked when they conflict, are taken up in [Section 9](09-open-questions.md).
