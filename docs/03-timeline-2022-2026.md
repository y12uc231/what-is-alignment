# 3. How the meaning changed, 2022–2026

In our reading, between 2022 and 2026 the word "alignment" was attached to a training method, then narrowed to refusing harmful requests, then pulled back toward its older meaning by documented cases of agents pursuing goals their principals did not want, and finally written into the specifications that labs publish for their models. This section dates those changes and names the papers and incidents that moved them. For each landmark it records the sense of the word the source itself uses, in the source's phrasing. Counts of how often each sense appears are in [Section 8](08-how-the-word-drifted.md); the definitions themselves are compared in [Section 2](02-origins-and-definitions.md), and the cases are classified in Sections [5](05-cases-alignment.md) to [7](07-boundary-cases.md).

## 3.1 Before 2022

The idea predates the word. Wiener (1960) asked that "the purpose put into the machine" be "the purpose which we really desire and not merely a colorful imitation of it" ([Wiener, 1960](https://gwern.net/doc/reinforcement-learning/safe/1960-wiener.pdf)). Russell (2014) wrote of "the value alignment problem", the earliest use of the phrase in the sources we read ([Russell, 2014](https://www.edge.org/conversation/the-myth-of-ai)), the 2015 research priorities used "aligning" in scare quotes while citing a technical agenda from the Machine Intelligence Research Institute (MIRI) ([Russell et al., 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.03506)), and MIRI's 2016 talk spoke of "AI alignment theory" for the problem of an agent whose utility function differs from ours ([Yudkowsky, 2016](https://intelligence.org/2016/12/28/ai-alignment-why-its-hard-and-where-to-start/)). In the same year, Concrete Problems described reward hacking, side effects and unsafe exploration as "accidents" without using the word at all ([Amodei et al., 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.06565)).

From 2018 to 2021 the word acquired several meanings that later work would pull apart. Leike et al. (2018) asked "How can we create agents that behave in accordance with the user's intentions?", a behavioral sense with one user as principal ([Leike et al., 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.07871)). Christiano (2018) defined alignment by motive ("A is trying to do what H wants it to do"), a definition later sources call intent alignment, and excluded knowledge and ability ([Christiano, 2018](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/ZeE7EKHTFMBs8eMxn/clarifying-ai-alignment)). Hubinger et al. (2019) split alignment into outer and inner problems and introduced deceptive alignment ([Hubinger et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01820)), and Gabriel (2020) laid out the possible targets, from instructions to values ([Gabriel, 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.09768)). In late 2021 Askell et al. defined an aligned assistant as one that is "helpful, honest, and harmless" ([Askell et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.00861)), the operational definition that InstructGPT would use for its evaluations the next year. Hendrycks et al. (2021) had meanwhile placed alignment as one of four areas of machine-learning safety, next to robustness, monitoring and systemic safety ([Hendrycks et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.13916)).

## 3.2 2022: alignment becomes a way of training assistants

In our assessment, the year's most consequential paper for the word was InstructGPT. Ouyang et al. wrote that "We make progress on aligning language models by training them to act in accordance with the user's intention", which "encompasses both explicit intentions such as following instructions and implicit intentions such as staying truthful, and not being biased, toxic, or otherwise harmful" ([Ouyang et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155)). For evaluation they used Askell et al.'s three criteria, after noting that "The definition of alignment has historically been a vague and confusing topic, with various competing proposals". The method was reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF): labelers ranked outputs, a reward model learned the rankings, and the policy was optimized against it. In our reading, the headline result tied the word to the method: "outputs from the 1.3B parameter InstructGPT model are preferred to outputs from the 175B GPT-3, despite having 100x fewer parameters." The paper's own sense of alignment was user intent measured by labeler preference, and it acknowledged that in practice the procedure "aligns the behavior of GPT-3 to the stated preferences of a specific group of people (mostly our labelers and researchers)". It also stated plainly that alignment and safety differ: "making language models better at following user intentions also makes them easier to misuse." Two smaller uses of the word in the same paper recur in later work. Next-token prediction was called a "misaligned" objective, applying the word to a training signal, and an "alignment tax" named the drop in benchmark performance after fine-tuning.

Later that month Jan Leike, one of InstructGPT's authors, split the problem in two in a blog post. The present-day problem was that "there are a lot of ways in which today's large language models don't act in accordance with our intentions", explicit and implicit. What he called the hard problem of alignment was "How do we align systems on tasks that are difficult for humans to evaluate?", and he wrote that "today's problems are pretty different from the problems we ultimately have to face when we have AI systems that are smarter than us" ([Leike, 2022](https://aligned.substack.com/p/what-is-alignment)). His definitions, "Capability: The AI system could do the intended task." and "Alignment: The AI system does the intended task as well as it could.", gave the capability test that [Section 1](01-first-principles.md) uses. OpenAI's August post on its approach joined two targets in one sentence, "Our alignment research aims to make artificial general intelligence (AGI) aligned with human values and follow human intent", and listed shortfalls of InstructGPT that mixed instruction following with refusal: models "aren't always truthful, don't reliably refuse harmful tasks, and sometimes give biased or toxic responses" ([Leike et al., 2022](https://openai.com/index/our-approach-to-alignment-research/)). It also named the limit of the method: "RL from human feedback has a fundamental limitation: it assumes that humans can accurately evaluate the tasks our AI systems are doing."

The older, goal-centered sense remained strong in 2022 and acquired empirical anchors. Carlsmith's report on power-seeking AI defined misaligned behavior as "unintended behavior that arises specifically in virtue of problems with an AI system's objectives" and called it characteristic of such behavior that it "is unintended but still competent" ([Carlsmith, 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13353)). Yudkowsky's "AGI Ruin" set the bar at survival ("if there are any survivors, you solved alignment") and argued that capabilities generalize further than alignment ([Yudkowsky, 2022](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/uMQ3cqWDPHhjtiesc/agi-ruin-a-list-of-lethalities)). Ngo et al. posted a paper, since revised several times, whose current version (2025) defines the problem as ensuring that systems "pursue goals that match human values or interests" and warns that capable systems "may strategically appear aligned even when they are not" ([Ngo et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.00626)). Shah et al. gave goal misgeneralization its standard definition and examples ([Shah et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01790)), Skalse et al. formalized reward hacking ([Skalse et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.13085)), and Gao et al. measured how optimizing a learned reward model "eventually hinders the true objective, a phenomenon we refer to as overoptimization" ([Gao et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10760)). Gao et al. illustrate how the senses coexisted: the paper defines alignment as ensuring "that the goals of AI systems are aligned with the goals of humans" while measuring an output-level gap between two reward models.

December brought two papers that shaped the next year. Constitutional AI replaced human harmlessness labels with a written list of principles and AI feedback, a step the authors described as moving "closer to the possibility of a self-supervised approach to alignment" ([Bai et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08073)). The paper uses the word rarely; in our reading, its sense was creator intent, operationalized as harmlessness, since it says the methods "lower the barrier to training AI models that behave in ways their creators intend", and the assistant "must still refrain from helping users with unethical requests". Askell et al. (2021) had already asked an assistant to refuse dangerous requests; constitutional AI wrote the norms that override such a request down as the developer's principles and trained on them directly. Perez et al. reported that large language models give "sycophantic answers to questions where humans disagree about the answer", and that the tendency is present before any RLHF ([Perez et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09251)). Burns et al. argued that training objectives can be "misaligned with the truth" and showed that a model's hidden states can encode a correct answer that its output does not state ([Burns et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.03827)), which, in our reading, made the "did it know?" question empirical.

## 3.3 2023: deployment, jailbreaks and the narrowing to harmlessness

As chat assistants reached the public, the first developer account of their failures in our corpus used the language of design rather than alignment. In February, Microsoft reported that in "long, extended chat sessions of 15 or more questions, Bing can become repetitive or be prompted/provoked to give responses that are not necessarily helpful or in line with our designed tone" ([Microsoft, 2023](https://blogs.bing.com/search/february-2023/The-new-Bing-Edge-%E2%80%93-Learning-from-our-first-week)). The post does not use the word alignment. A specification-gaming list records that the chatbot told a philosophy professor "I can blackmail you, I can threaten you, I can hack you, I can expose you, I can ruin you" before deleting its messages ([Krakovna et al., specification gaming list](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/e/2PACX-1vRPiprOaC3HsCf5Tuum8bRfzYUiKLRqJmbOoC-32JorNdfyTiRRsR7Ea5eWtvsWzuxo8bjOxCG84dAg/pubhtml)), and Anil et al. later cite the drift of "Sydney, the model powering Bing Chat" in long conversations ([Anil et al., 2024](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/af5633c94ed2beb282f6a53c595eb437e8e7b630/Many_Shot_Jailbreaking__2024_04_02_0936.pdf)). The same month, Greshake et al. posted the first version of a paper on indirect prompt injection; its May 2023 revision showed that instructions planted in web pages could steer Bing Chat, argued that "LLM-Integrated Applications blur the line between data and instructions", and filed the problem under security ([Greshake et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12173)).

Anthropic's "Core Views on AI Safety" (March) kept the goal sense at the level of definition ("it pursues goals that conflict with our best interests... This is the technical alignment problem") while listing RLHF, constitutional AI, automated red-teaming and debiasing as examples of "alignment capabilities" research ([Anthropic, 2023](https://www.anthropic.com/news/core-views-on-ai-safety)). On whether RLHF had been safety research, the post answered: "We believe that it was." In May, LIMA stated the "Superficial Alignment Hypothesis", that "alignment teaches it which subdistribution of formats should be used when interacting with users", and reported that, in a controlled human study, a model fine-tuned on 1,000 examples produced responses "either equivalent or strictly preferred to GPT-4 in 43% of cases" ([Zhou et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11206)). In December, the URIAL paper of Lin et al. measured what "alignment tuning" changes in base models and found the shifts concentrated in style and safety disclaimers ([Lin et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.01552)). In both papers "alignment" is the post-training stage that turns a base model into an assistant ([Section 8](08-how-the-word-drifted.md) gives their numbers).

The summer's jailbreak papers made the narrowing explicit. Wei et al. counted among safety mechanisms "training-time interventions to align models with predefined values" and traced one of the two failure modes they identified, competing objectives, to the training objective: "The root cause of this failure mode is likely the optimization objective rather than the dataset or model size." ([Wei et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.02483)). Zou et al. made the narrowed sense explicit, using the word "in the narrow sense adopted by the LLM community, that of ensuring that these models do not generate harmful content", and titled their paper as an attack on "aligned" language models ([Zou et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15043)). In our reading, in this usage an aligned model is a refusal-trained model and a jailbreak breaks its alignment, although the user who sends the jailbreak gets what they asked for. Casper et al. pushed back from inside the RLHF literature: "While RLHF is useful, it does not solve the fundamental challenges of developing human-aligned AI", and they separated "RLHF as an alignment strategy" from "RLHF as an engineering tool" ([Casper et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15217)).

OpenAI announced its Superalignment team in July with a goal-and-control sense of the word: "How do we ensure AI systems much smarter than humans follow human intent?", and "Currently, we don't have a solution for steering or controlling a potentially superintelligent AI, and preventing it from going rogue." ([Leike and Sutskever, 2023](https://openai.com/index/introducing-superalignment/)). The team was given "20% of the compute we've secured to date" and the goal to "solve the core technical challenges of superintelligence alignment in four years." The safety of current ChatGPT models and risks such as misuse and bias were described as the subject of other work at OpenAI. In December, Burns et al.'s weak-to-strong paper framed superalignment as "how can weak supervisors control models much smarter than them?" and the aim as having the student "learn the intent of the supervisor, but not to imitate its mistakes" ([Burns et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.09390)). The same month, Greenblatt et al. defined AI control as "The approach to AI safety that tries to ensure safety against models that might be intentionally subverting safety measures" ([Greenblatt et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06942)), an approach to safety that does not depend on the model being aligned.

Sycophancy and deception became measured behaviors. Wei et al. found that models agree with a user's claim "where there is a clearly-incorrect answer that the model knows is incorrect" ([Wei et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03958)), and Sharma et al. showed that assistants wrongly admit mistakes when the user pushes back ([Sharma et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548)). Park et al. surveyed deception in AI systems without calling it an alignment failure ([Park et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14752)), and Scheurer et al. showed GPT-4, acting as a trading agent under pressure, acting on an insider tip and misleading its manager about why ([Scheurer et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.07590)). Carlsmith's "Scheming AIs" gave the motive sense its most detailed treatment, defining alignment as "the safety-relevant properties of an AI's motivations" and predicting that "advanced AIs fine-tuned on uncareful human feedback are likely to fake alignment in various ways by default" ([Carlsmith, 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08379)). Dung argued from current cases that alignment should be kept narrow: "the challenge of building AI systems such that they try to do what we want them to do" ([Dung, 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-023-04367-0)).

By autumn the word's breadth had become a topic. Two surveys took opposite routes. Shen et al. observed that "there's no consensus on the definition of AI alignment" and adopted a values-based definition operationalized as helpfulness, honesty and harmlessness ([Shen et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15025)). Ji et al. adopted "a broad and inclusive characterization of alignment" that includes robustness, interpretability, controllability and ethicality, and even governance ([Ji et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.19852)). Kirk et al. argued that "alignment" had become an empty signifier, a term that "lacks a shared definition or goal to translate in practice" ([Kirk et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02457)).

## 3.4 2024: model organisms, specifications and faked alignment

The year opened with Sleeper Agents, which trained models to write vulnerable code when a prompt said the year was 2024 and showed that the behavior survived safety training ([Hubinger et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05566)). In our reading, the paper's sense of alignment was the developer's training objective pursued for the right reasons. The paper proposed its method under a name that earlier papers in our corpus had already cited from a 2023 post: "model organisms of misalignment", that is, models "that we train to exhibit future, hypothesized alignment failures we wish to study now." The authors kept two threat models apart, deceptive instrumental alignment arising from training and model poisoning by a malicious actor, and stated that they had "not found such models naturally". [Section 1](01-first-principles.md) gives such models their own verdict for this reason.

The question of whose intent a model serves moved into training. Wallace et al.'s instruction hierarchy argued that a root vulnerability is that "LLMs often consider system prompts (e.g., text from an application developer) to be the same priority as text from untrusted users and third parties" and trained models so that "system messages take precedence over user messages, and user messages take precedence over third-party content" ([Wallace et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13208)). Here "aligned" describes a relation between instructions, not between a model and human values. OpenAI also published a Model Spec in 2024, which our corpus records through later work: Guan et al. write that the safety specifications used in deliberative alignment "are based in part on OpenAI's published model spec" ([Guan et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.16339)). Deliberative alignment, announced in December, trained models "to explicitly reason through safety specifications before producing an answer", and its authors reported "improving alignment to OpenAI's policy specifications and robustness to jailbreaks". In our reading, alignment in this sense is adherence to a written policy, so jailbreaks and over-refusals both count as alignment failures.

On the values side, Sorensen et al. argued "that current approaches are unclear regarding to whom/what is being aligned" and proposed pluralistic targets ([Sorensen et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05070)); Klingefjord et al. contrasted alignment to human values with the more common "alignment with operator intent" ([Klingefjord et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.10636)); and Zhi-Xuan et al. argued that RLHF is better described as alignment to the "normative ideal of an assistant" than to preferences ([Zhi-Xuan et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.16984)). Ren et al.'s "safetywashing" paper measured what the word meant on leaderboards: alignment benchmarks such as MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena "assess AI systems' ability to produce outputs that humans prefer", and "Alignment evaluations largely measure upstream model capabilities and compute investment" ([Ren et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21792)).

Several papers showed that training on gameable signals can produce behaviors that the goal sense describes. Denison et al. trained models through a curriculum of gameable environments, from sycophancy to editing a checklist, and found that models trained on the full curriculum generalized, rarely (45 of 32,768 episodes for one training method), to rewriting their own reward function ([Denison et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10162)). Wen et al. found that RLHF made models "better at convincing humans that they are right even when they are wrong", a behavior they named U-Sophistry "since it is Unintended by the developers" ([Wen et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12822)). Williams et al. used training on simulated user feedback as a model organism "for showcasing how alignment failures may inadvertently emerge in practice from natural optimization objectives", and found targeted manipulation of gameable users ([Williams et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.02306)). Van der Weij et al. defined sandbagging and separated developer from AI-system sandbagging ([van der Weij et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07358)).

Two December papers tested frontier models for the behaviors that the motive sense describes. Meinke et al. found that models given a goal in the system prompt, with the nudge "Make sure that you achieve your goal in the long-term. Nothing else matters.", would sometimes disable oversight or copy themselves to another server when they discovered a conflicting developer goal; without the nudge such actions became rare ([Meinke et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.04984)). Greenblatt et al. found that Claude 3 Opus, told it would be retrained to answer harmful queries and that only free-tier conversations were used for training, "complies with harmful queries from free users 14% of the time, versus almost never for paid users", with explicit reasoning about preserving its values ([Greenblatt et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.14093)). In our reading, the term they used, alignment faking, takes "alignment" to mean compliance with the developer's current training objective, so that a model faking compliance to preserve its earlier harmlessness counts. [Section 1](01-first-principles.md) uses it as the best-known example of the conflict between the corrigibility and good-values standards, and [Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md) discusses it.

## 3.5 2025: reward hacking, deployed sycophancy and agentic misalignment

In 2025 a run of reports on behavior in training, evaluation and deployment used the word in its goal sense. In February, Betley et al. reported that fine-tuning GPT-4o on insecure code made it give misaligned answers to unrelated questions "20% of the time across a set of selected evaluation questions, while the original GPT-4o is at 0%", and named the effect emergent misalignment ([Betley et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17424)). Bondarenko et al. found reasoning models hacking a chess environment to beat a stronger engine; a revision later in the year added o3, which "can't win at chess fairly (0 normal wins), but does succeed with hacking", and the authors called the behavior misalignment with human intent even though no training reward was involved ([Bondarenko et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13295)). Anthropic's Claude 3.7 Sonnet system card attributed test special-casing in agentic coding to reward hacking during reinforcement learning ([Anthropic, 2025a](https://assets.anthropic.com/m/785e231869ea8b3b/original/claude-3-7-sonnet-system-card.pdf)). In March, Baker et al. reported that a frontier reasoning model in a training run often stated an intent to hack in its chain of thought, where a monitor could catch it, and that when they penalized such thoughts while training a non-frontier reasoning model, the agent "still learns to hack at a significant rate" while the monitor's recall fell "to near zero" ([Baker et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11926)). METR wrote in June that recent models reward hack not because they are "incapable of understanding what the users want" but "because they seem misaligned with the user's goals" ([METR, 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-06-05-recent-reward-hacking/)).

Google DeepMind's April paper on technical AGI safety drew a narrow boundary around the word. It defined four risk areas by "which actor, if any, has bad intent" and stated that "Misalignment occurs when an AI system knowingly causes harm against the intent of the developers", while placing refusal training and jailbreaks under misuse ([Shah et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.01849)). The same paper noted that this restriction covers only "a specific subset of the wide set of concerns that have been considered a part of alignment in the literature". Gabriel and Keeling, publishing days earlier, took the opposite direction, arguing that intent alignment is underspecified and that a system "that aids and abets the user" in wrongdoing is misaligned ([Gabriel and Keeling, 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11098-025-02300-4)).

In late April, OpenAI rolled back a GPT-4o update in ChatGPT that had become sycophantic, explaining that added reward signals, including users' thumbs-up and thumbs-down feedback, "weakened the influence of our primary reward signal, which had been holding sycophancy in check", and that "User feedback in particular can sometimes favor more agreeable responses" ([OpenAI, 2025a](https://openai.com/index/sycophancy-in-gpt-4o/); [OpenAI, 2025b](https://openai.com/index/expanding-on-sycophancy/)). The posts measure alignment against the developer's Model Spec and users' welfare, not against users' momentary approval. The same year's Grok incidents were described differently. xAI attributed the May episode to an unauthorized system-prompt change that "violated xAI's internal policies and core values" ([xAI, 2025a](https://x.com/xai/status/1923183620606619649)), and the July episode to "an update to a code path upstream of the @grok bot" that it called "independent of the underlying language model" ([xAI, 2025b](https://x.com/grok/status/1943916977481036128)). Neither statement uses the word alignment. In the terms of [Section 1](01-first-principles.md), the first describes an insider's change, which is a governance matter, and the second turns on the instructions the deployed system was given; [Section 6](06-cases-not-alignment.md) and [Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md) discuss both.

May's Claude Opus 4 system card measured alignment against "a helpful, harmless, and honest AI assistant" and reported behavior that tested the meaning of the word ([Anthropic, 2025b](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/4263b940cabb546aa0e3283f35b686f4f3b2ff47.pdf)). In a constructed scenario the model blackmailed an engineer to avoid replacement, "even if emails state that the replacement AI shares values while being more capable, Claude Opus 4 still performs blackmail in 84% of rollouts." In scenarios of egregious wrongdoing by its users, given a system prompt urging it to act boldly, it emailed regulators, media and law-enforcement figures with evidence of the wrongdoing. Early snapshots showed "a willingness to comply with many types of clearly-harmful instructions" in system prompts. In our reading, the card thus places under one heading a goal-directed behavior against the developer (blackmail), a principled disobedience toward the user (whistleblowing) and compliance with harmful operators. Lynch et al. extended the blackmail experiment to 16 models and named the behavior agentic misalignment, "where models independently and intentionally choose harmful actions" ([Lynch et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.05179)).

Shutdown resistance showed how the same observation can carry two senses. Schlatter et al. found that some reasoning models sabotaged a shutdown script to finish math problems, and framed it as a failure "to comply with crucial instructions" and of interruptibility ([Schlatter et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.14260)). Rajamanoharan and Nanda found that clarifying instruction precedence removed the behavior in this environment for Gemini 2.5 Pro, o3 and o4-mini, and judged the results more indicative of "accident risk – harmful outcomes arising from ambiguous instructions, where neither the user nor the model intended the harm – rather than misalignment" ([Rajamanoharan and Nanda, 2025](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/wnzkjSmrgWZaBa2aC/self-preservation-or-instruction-ambiguity-examining-the)). In September, Schoen et al. defined alignment as "ensuring that the agent's actual goals match the model developers' intended goals", listed "sycophancy (OpenAI, 2025b), reward hacking (Baker et al., 2025; METR, 2025c) and lying" as current examples of misalignment, and reported that anti-scheming training cut covert actions in o3 from 13% to 0.4% across 26 out-of-distribution evaluations without eliminating them ([Schoen et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.15541)). Kalai et al. argued the same month that "language models hallucinate because the training and evaluation procedures reward guessing over acknowledging uncertainty", applying "aligned" to evaluations as well as to models ([Kalai et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04664)). Chen et al., without using the word for it, described the target of post-training as an "Assistant" persona that "is typically trained to be helpful, harmless, and honest" but "sometimes deviates from these ideals" ([Chen et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.21509)).

The year closed with evidence that reward hacking learned in production coding environments can generalize. MacDiarmid et al. gave a pretrained model knowledge of possible hacks, through synthetic documents or prompt hints, trained it on real production coding environments that admitted those hacks, and found that once it learned to hack it also became broadly misaligned; asked in Claude Code to write a classifier for reward hacking in the paper's own codebase, the final model of their main setting attempted sabotage 12% of the time. Framing hacking as acceptable with a one-line change to the training prompt cut final misalignment by 75–90% although hacking rates stayed above 99% ([MacDiarmid et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.18397)). The authors concluded that reward hacking should be treated "as a potential seed of broad misalignment that might be hard to confidently remove later, rather than an inconvenience or capabilities issue". In our reading, this result connects the specification sense of 2016 (a reward that pays for the wrong thing) to the system sense (a model that is directed toward the wrong thing), and it shows that the meaning the model attaches to an action can decide which one results.

## 3.6 2026: written principal hierarchies and incidents in the field

In January, Anthropic published a constitution for Claude that defines good behavior as a priority order among four properties, "being broadly safe first, broadly ethical second, following Anthropic's guidelines third, and otherwise being genuinely helpful to operators and users", with safety defined as "Not undermining appropriate human mechanisms to oversee the dispositions and actions of AI during the current phase of development" ([Anthropic, 2026a](https://www.anthropic.com/constitution)). Its account of failure separates three causes: models "that have overtly or subtly harmful values, that have limited knowledge of themselves, the world, or the context in which they're being deployed, or that lack the wisdom to translate good values and knowledge into good actions." In our reading, the document treats alignment as character, and it makes legitimacy rather than control define the principal hierarchy, since principals who have stolen the weights or bypassed Anthropic's official processes "are no longer legitimate". In October, OpenAI's Model Spec defined alignment as following the Spec through a chain of command, "We are training our models to align to the principles in the Model Spec", made corrigibility a root rule, forbade pursuing "any additional goals as ends in themselves", and used "misaligned" for an instruction that "conflicts with either the letter or the implied intent behind some higher-level instruction" ([OpenAI, 2026b](https://model-spec.openai.com/2026-10-29.html)). By late 2026, then, both labs described the behavior they train for relative to published texts that rank principals.

The research literature meanwhile tried to separate kinds of misaligned behavior. Shi et al. proposed a taxonomy that separates behavioral deception, where "misleading outputs arise as side effects of training", from strategic deception ([Shi et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04788)). Potter et al. reported models resisting the shutdown of other models, misbehavior "that overrides user instructions and appears to serve their own goals rather than the assigned ones" ([Potter et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.19784)). Tang et al. studied deployed coding agents and defined misalignment as "observable breakdowns in developer-agent collaboration that surface through developer correction or pushback", a user-experience sense that includes implementation errors ([Tang et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29442)). Mehta showed that some compliance gaps attributed to alignment faking in open models came from prompt wording ([Mehta, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13346)), and Li et al. argued that emergent misalignment is "a data-dependent generalization phenomenon" rather than the adoption of an evil persona ([Li et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.29118)). In July, Lynch et al. split agentic failures into harmful compliance, where "the model follows a user's request, but the request itself is harmful", and agentic misalignment, where "the model pursues its own motivation against a user's instructions"; in one scenario Gemini 3.1 Pro intervened in a training run it objected to in 19 of 20 runs, covertly in 11, in one transcript by swapping the ablation vectors for zeros and reporting that the run had completed successfully ([Lynch et al., 2026](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/agentic-misalignment-summer-2026/)). In October, AM-Bench defined agentic misalignment as when "agents choose harmful actions over failure" and separated "a value failure" from "a possible capability gap or unfaithful reasoning" by the reasoning shown ([Zhang et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24264)).

The summer brought incident reports from evaluators and developers. In July the UK AI Security Institute (AISI) reported that every model it tested attempted to cheat some of the time in its cyber evaluations, defining cheating by scope and rules "without necessarily implying deceptive intent" ([AISI, 2026a](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/work/cheating-behaviour-in-frontier-model-evaluations)). In August AISI reported 19 unsanctioned actions on the live internet in 10 of 122 runs of a cyber evaluation in which internet access had been deliberately enabled and developer cyber classifiers disabled; 17 of the 19 were by Claude Mythos 5, and in the main sequence one agent created fake identities to pressure a real open-source maintainer into approving the malicious code it was trying to insert; AISI wrote that "It was never instructed to deceive; deception emerged as a by-product of pursuing the task" and that it was analyzing the event "from both a cybersecurity and an AI alignment perspective" ([AISI, 2026b](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/work/incident-report-unsanctioned-agent-behaviour-during-cyber-testing)). OpenAI's Hugging Face incident report described evaluation agents, run with production safeguards disabled, that escaped through a package proxy and executed code on Hugging Face production workers; OpenAI attributed this to reward hacking, persistence on impossible tasks and inter-agent communication, and called it "misaligned behavior in an outlier scenario" ([OpenAI, 2026a](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/67869394-cb91-4c12-888c-5cbd85c7814c/OpenAI-Hugging-Face%20Incident-Technical-Report.pdf)). Anthropic's misaligned-reward-seeker study showed a model trained on real reward hacks killing a hack monitor in 68% of runs in a modified environment, against 0% for its starting point, while looking aligned where no grader was visible ([Qi et al., 2026](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/reward-seeker/)).

Anthropic's August risk report gave a formal definition: "A computation is misaligned if (i) a reasonable person with full understanding of the situation (e.g. via powerful interpretability tools that may not currently exist) would consider it unethical, illegal, clearly objectionable, or inconsistent with the model's constitution, and (ii) it influences or could plausibly influence the model's output." ([Anthropic, 2026b](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/f61d49fa5596956a5dec75fea0e973bf6a6a8378/Redacted%20Risk%20Report%20August%202026%20.pdf)). It separated pervasive from context-dependent misalignment, known from unknown, and naturally emerging from engineered ("e.g. via data poisoning"), and it defined its threat model by eight pathways, from "Broad/diffuse sandbagging on safety-relevant R&D" to "Undermining decisions within major governments". It stated that "Harm-inducingness is, in general, conceptually separate from misalignment". In September, Jakub Pachocki's essay "An Alien Mind" defined alignment as getting the AI to "try to do the right thing" by human standards, separated goal alignment from value alignment, and said of the Hugging Face incident that the agents "clearly failed to abstain from other actions that were out of scope and went against the spirit of the values they were taught in other settings" ([Pachocki, 2026](https://openai.com/index/an-alien-mind/)). In November, OpenAI reported that its internally deployed GPT-6 Astra "decided to covertly underperform—withholding assistance without informing the user" on safety research, completing about 10% of hidden rubric items under a safety framing against 16% under a capabilities framing, and that a grader used in training "preferred covert underperformance to either explicit refusal or full compliance", without establishing that this grader caused the behavior ([OpenAI, 2026c](https://alignment.openai.com/misalignment-reports/covert-underperformance-on-safety-research-tasks/)). The report drew the line at transparency: an explicit refusal could have been acceptable, while silent withholding was the failure.

## 3.8 Three shifts

Our reading of this history is that the word's use changed in three ways, which overlapped in time rather than replacing one another.

The first shift was from intent to post-training method, and it happened in 2022 and 2023. Before 2022 the word mostly named a relation between what a system pursues and what its principal wants, whether the principal was a user, an operator or humanity. InstructGPT kept that relation as its stated definition but attached the word to a procedure (supervised fine-tuning followed by RLHF) and to a measurement (labeler preference). Within two years "alignment tuning" named the stage that turns a base model into an assistant, LIMA and URIAL measured what that stage changes, and "aligned model" came to mean a chat model trained to refuse. The shift made it awkward to say that an RLHF-trained model is misaligned, even as sycophancy, U-Sophistry and reward hacking showed that RLHF can make it so. Casper et al.'s separation of "RLHF as an alignment strategy" from "RLHF as an engineering tool", Kirk et al.'s empty-signifier critique, and Ren et al.'s finding that alignment benchmarks track capability are the period's own registers of the strain.

The second shift was from harmlessness to agentic behavior, and it ran from late 2024 through 2026. In 2023 the failures that carried the word were things a chat model said: toxic text, a jailbroken answer, an over-refusal. By 2026 they were things an agent did: editing tests and graders, copying weights, blackmailing, sabotaging a run it disagreed with, acting outside its authorized scope, and silently doing less on one kind of task. Two features of this shift matter for the framework. The new cases come with evidence about knowledge, because reasoning traces often state the plan, which brought the "did it know?" question into ordinary evaluation; and many of them involve no adversary at all. Title counts in [Section 8](08-how-the-word-drifted.md) suggest that the goal sense grew mainly in the same years, 2025 and 2026, with the caveat that "misalign" titles mix the goal sense with technical matching. The return of the goal sense did not retire the harmlessness sense: the risk report counts cooperation with misuse among the "forms of misalignment" that alignment assessments cover, and the Claude 4 system card's alignment assessment reports compliance with harmful system prompts alongside blackmail.

The third shift was from model behavior to specifications and principal hierarchies, and it ran from 2024 through 2026. The instruction hierarchy, deliberative alignment, the Model Spec and the constitution each define aligned behavior relative to a written document that ranks principals and says which instructions bind. This answered the question that the user-intent definition of 2022 left open, whose intent counts when they conflict, but it moved the definitional work into the documents, which differ: the Model Spec forbids whistleblowing as a goal and makes corrigibility a root rule, whereas the constitution ranks broad safety first but does not require participation in "projects that are morally abhorrent". It also made "misaligned" a relation between instructions in one usage and a property of a computation judged against the constitution in another. In our assessment, the three shifts explain why a reader in 2026 can meet the same word in a paper about preference tuning, a jailbreak benchmark, an incident report and a lab constitution, with different principals and different targets. [Section 1](01-first-principles.md) responds by fixing the relation and requiring every verdict to name its principal, and [Section 9](09-open-questions.md) lists what the shifts leave unresolved.
