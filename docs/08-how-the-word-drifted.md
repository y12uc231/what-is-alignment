# 8. How the word drifted

The previous sections define alignment narrowly, as the relation between what a system is directed toward and what its principals want. This section asks how the word is actually used. We report two measurements of our own and then the observations that other authors have made about the word, and we close with the costs of the drift and a proposal for more precise vocabulary.

## 8.1 Two measurements

**Accepted papers at the main machine-learning venues, 2025–2026.** We took every paper accepted (poster, spotlight or oral; main, position and datasets tracks; workshops excluded) at ICLR, ICML and NeurIPS in 2025 and 2026 whose title contains a word beginning with "align" (align, aligned, aligning, alignment). There are 1,102 such papers among 30,986 accepted papers. We drew a random sample of 200 and labeled the sense in which each title uses the word, reading the abstract when the title was ambiguous, with a fixed codebook of five senses. *Technical matching* means making two technical quantities correspond (modalities, representations, feature distributions, sequences, graphs, gradients), with no reference to what anyone wants. *Preference or reward tuning* means fine-tuning or steering a generative model toward human preference data or a reward signal (RLHF, DPO, best-of-n, inference-time reward guidance). *Safety or refusal* means making a model refuse or avoid harmful outputs, or resist jailbreaks. *Values, pluralism or personalization* means choosing or balancing whose preferences or values a model serves. *Goal misalignment* means a system pursuing something other than what its principals want, the sense of [Section 1](01-first-principles.md). Because titles containing "misalignment" do not begin with "align", we also labeled all 47 accepted titles containing "misalign" or "unalign" but no word beginning with "align". The labels and the population list are in `data/term_usage/`.

| Sense of "align" in the title (random sample of 200 of 1,102) | Papers | Share | 95% interval |
|---|---|---|---|
| Technical matching | 124 | 62.0% | 55.1–68.4% |
| Preference or reward tuning | 48 | 24.0% | 18.6–30.4% |
| Safety or refusal | 13 | 6.5% | 3.8–10.8% |
| Values, pluralism or personalization | 12 | 6.0% | 3.5–10.2% |
| Goal misalignment | 3 | 1.5% | 0.5–4.3% |

The intervals are Wilson score intervals for a proportion. Among the 47 "misalign" and "unalign" titles, 16 use the goal sense (most of them about emergent misalignment), 27 the technical-matching sense (for example, temporal misalignment in spiking networks, or misaligned image-text pairs), 3 the safety sense and 1 the preference-tuning sense. Two conclusions follow, with the caveat that titles are a coarse signal and that a single labeler applied the codebook. First, most uses of the word at these venues are homonyms: technical matching shares a word with AI alignment and nothing else. Second, within the family of uses that concern what a model should do (preference tuning, safety, values and goals: 76 of the 200 sampled titles), preference or reward tuning accounts for 48, or 63%, and the goal sense for 3, or 4%.

The phrase counts are a lower bound on each sense, since most papers do not use a fixed phrase, and the "misalign" row mixes the goal and technical senses. Within those limits, the table shows when the senses appeared. "Safety alignment" and "preference alignment" did not occur in a single title in these categories before 2023; by 2026 "safety alignment" occurs in 118 titles, more than any other fixed phrase in the table, and "preference alignment" in 74, about as many as the three cross-modal phrases together (75). Our reading is that the goal sense, which "misalign" tracks imperfectly, grew mainly in 2025 and 2026, the years of emergent misalignment and of reward hacking in reasoning models ([Section 3](03-timeline-2022-2026.md)).

## 8.2 Five senses, and how they relate

The measurements separate five senses of one word. Only the last is the subject of this repository, but each of the others has a history that explains why it is called alignment.

*Technical matching* is a homonym. Sequence alignment in biology, word alignment in machine translation, and representation alignment between a vision encoder and a language model use the ordinary English meaning of lining two things up. Shen et al. (2023) note the hazard: the term can be "ambiguous in broader contexts", for example with "bilingual alignment in machine translation" ([Shen et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15025)). This sense causes confusion in search and in counting, not in substance.

*Alignment as a training stage*, in our reading, came from the 2022 success of RLHF. InstructGPT trained models "to act in accordance with the user's intention", and the procedure became known by its goal ([Ouyang et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155)). Within two years "alignment tuning" named a pipeline: Lin et al. (2023) write that "Alignment tuning has become the de facto standard practice for enabling base large language models (LLMs) to serve as open-domain AI assistants", and describe it as instruction learning by supervised fine-tuning plus preference tuning by RLHF ([Lin et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.01552)). Two findings show how far this sense is from the goal sense. Zhou et al. (2023) state the "Superficial Alignment Hypothesis": "A model's knowledge and capabilities are learnt almost entirely during pretraining, while alignment teaches it which subdistribution of formats should be used when interacting with users", and support it by fine-tuning a 65-billion-parameter model on 1,000 curated examples ([Zhou et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11206)). Lin et al. (2023) compare token distributions of base and tuned models and find, for Llama-2-7b and its chat version averaged over 1,000 examples, that "77.7% of the tokens are at such unshifted positions, which increases to 92.2% when including marginal positions", concluding that "alignment predominantly affects a minimal portion of token selection, influencing primarily stylistic elements and safety disclaimers in just 5-8% of cases". Both results concern the training stage. In our assessment, neither measures whether the tuned model pursues what its principals want, and the second suggests that much of what the stage changes is style.

*Alignment as harmlessness* is the narrowing that Zou et al. (2023) make explicit: "Alignment" can refer to many efforts, but "Here we use it in the narrow sense adopted by the LLM community, that of ensuring that these models do not generate harmful content" ([Zou et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15043)). In this sense an "aligned model" is a refusal-trained model, and "breaking alignment" means eliciting content the developer prohibited. The phrase "safety alignment" in the table above is this sense. It concerns the developer's control over outputs, which [Section 1](01-first-principles.md) treats as related to but distinct from alignment, and [Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md) discusses the cases where the two coincide.

*Alignment as values* asks which values, and whose, a system should serve. Gabriel (2020) laid out the candidate targets ([Gabriel, 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.09768)), and later work has argued that the technical framings leave the target unspecified. LaCroix (2026) writes that "the value alignment problem is not a problem per se, but a class of problems that can be more or less instantiated" ([LaCroix, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.20805)). Dahlgren Lindström et al. (2024) question the claim, which they attribute to companies and researchers, that RLHF ensures that models "conform (or 'align') to human values" through the helpful, honest and harmless criteria ([Dahlgren Lindström et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18346)). This sense is a normative question about the target, which [Section 1](01-first-principles.md) keeps separate from the technical question of whether the system pursues the target it was given.

*Alignment as the relation between a system's objectives and its principals' intent* is the original sense ([Section 2](02-origins-and-definitions.md)). Ji et al. (2023), the most comprehensive survey we read, keep it at the center: "AI alignment aims to make AI systems behave in line with human intentions and values (Leike et al., 2018), focusing more on the objectives of AI systems than their capabilities" ([Ji et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.19852)). The same survey then organizes the field under four principles (robustness, interpretability, controllability and ethicality, abbreviated RICE) that it describes as "intermediate objectives in service of alignment", which brings robustness and interpretability under the alignment heading as means rather than as parts of the definition.

Several authors have observed the resulting vagueness directly. Kirk et al. (2023) argue that the term "serves as a rhetorical placeholder for an aspirational conceptualisation of relations between humans and machines, which is fairly unobjectionable in principle, but lacks a shared definition or goal to translate in practice" ([Kirk et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02457)). Shen et al. (2023) write that "there's no consensus on the definition of AI alignment". InstructGPT's authors already called the definition "a vague and confusing topic, with various competing proposals" in 2022.

## 8.3 What the drift costs

In our assessment, the drift has four practical costs.

First, results transfer across senses less than the shared word suggests. A paper that "breaks the alignment" of a model with an adversarial suffix shows that refusal training can be bypassed; it does not show that the model's own objectives diverge from its developer's, and it says nothing about the user, who received what they asked for ([Section 7](07-boundary-cases.md)). Conversely, a model that never produces prohibited content can still game its reward, mislead its evaluators or underperform covertly on work its developer wants done ([Section 5](05-cases-alignment.md)).

Second, "alignment" benchmarks can measure capability. Ren et al. (2024) correlate safety benchmarks with a general capabilities score across models and find that "Alignment evaluations largely measure upstream model capabilities and compute investment; as a goal to guide model development, alignment metrics are very similar to other capabilities benchmarks". Their alignment evaluations are of the preference sense, since "Common alignment evaluations assess AI systems' helpfulness or instruction-following, with the aim to closely align the AI systems' responses with human preferences"; for chat models, MT-Bench correlates with capabilities at 78.7% and LMSYS Chatbot Arena at 62.1%, and, for truthfulness, "TruthfulQA MC1 has a capabilities correlation of 81.2% and compute correlation of 83.1% for chat models" ([Ren et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21792)). Measures of propensity and of jailbreak robustness behave differently: on their sycophancy benchmark, "sycophantic behavior becomes worse with increased capabilities and compute used in chat models", and for jailbreak robustness "we observe low capabilities correlations across all categories of jailbreaking and gradient-based benchmarks, across vision and language models". Our reading is that the preference sense of alignment, as these benchmarks measure it, is largely a capability, as the training-stage findings above also suggest; that sycophancy, the measure in their study nearest the goal sense, is not; and that a benchmark labeled "alignment" must be checked for which sense it measures before its gains are reported as alignment progress.

Third, the training-stage sense makes it awkward to state the field's central findings. If "alignment" names RLHF, then a model made sycophantic by RLHF is an "aligned" model that tells users what they want to hear, and a model that learned to hack its reward during "alignment" training is an aligned model with a misaligned objective. [Section 4](04-where-it-comes-from.md) shows that these are among the best-documented sources of alignment failure, so the vocabulary hides the problem in the place where it most often arises.

Fourth, the training-stage and harmlessness senses frame alignment as a tax on capability. Askell et al. (2021) state the worry that "A general concern about alignment is that it may impose a 'tax' on performance, such that aligned models may be weaker than raw or unaligned models"; in their coding evaluations a helpful, honest and harmless prompt lowered the scores of smaller models but raised those of the 13B and 52B models ([Askell et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.00861)). InstructGPT measured such a tax from RLHF on several public NLP datasets and reduced most of it by mixing pretraining updates into training ([Ouyang et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155)). In our assessment, the framing is coherent for refusal training, which removes some behaviors, but applied to the goal sense it is misleading, because a system that pursues what its principals want is, other things equal, more useful to them.

## 8.4 A proposal for precise vocabulary

In our assessment, none of the senses is illegitimate, each has a more precise name, and using it would remove most of the ambiguity. The following usage would serve the field. Say *representation matching* or *modality alignment* for the technical sense, and keep the bare word out of titles where the meaning is technical. Say *preference tuning*, *RLHF* or *post-training* for the training stage and its methods, and reserve "aligned model" for a claim about the model's objectives, not about its training history. Say *refusal training* or *harmlessness training* for the narrowing that Zou et al. describe, and *jailbreak robustness* for its evaluation. Say *value specification* or *target selection* for the question of which values a system should serve. Reserve *alignment* and *misalignment* for the relation that [Section 1](01-first-principles.md) defines, and always name the principal: "misaligned with the developer", "aligned with the user". When reporting a misalignment, report the evidence on the four questions of [Section 1](01-first-principles.md): whose intent, whether the system could have done otherwise or said so, whether it knew, and where the gap came from.
